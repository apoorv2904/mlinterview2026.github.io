Kaplan et al. — Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models

This tab was previously sourced entirely through Chinchilla — the paper arguing Kaplan is wrong. It is now checked against Kaplan's own PDF, and the loss exponents that were deliberately held empty are filled in from Table 5.

Two things stay second-hand on purpose, because they are claims about Kaplan made by another paper, and both come from a tab already verified: the exact-FLOPs-to-6ND ratio of 0.99–1.10 is Chinchilla Table A4, and the 130B-token schedule length is Chinchilla §2. Kaplan's own setup independently corroborates the second — see what it got wrong.

Not verifiable here: anything attributed to Switch, ST-MoE or Sardana et al. Those live in their own tabs.

The claim

Loss falls as a power law in each of model size N, data D and compute C, with the other two not bottlenecking. Architecture details — depth vs width, head count, feed-forward ratio — matter far less than scale. This is the paper that launched the race to larger models.

How weakly shape matters, with numbers (§3.1, Fig. 5): at fixed non-embedding N, the aspect ratio dmodel/nlayer can vary by a factor of 40 while loss moves only a few percent, and a (nlayer, dmodel) = (6, 4288) model lands within 3% of the (48, 1600) GPT-2 shape. Fig. 5 also prices the trade: 22% additional compute compensates for a 1% loss increase.

⚠️ Trap — six, seven or eight orders of magnitude

The abstract and §1 both say seven. The §1.1 summary bullet headed "Smooth power laws" says six. The precise version, stated under Fig. 4, is per variable: eight orders of magnitude in Cmin, six in N, over two in D. Quote the last one — it is the only version that says which variable, and the D range being the narrowest is the interesting part.

⚠️ Trap — the compute exponent has three values in one paper

Eq. 1.3 and Table 5 give the fitted αCmin = 0.050. §6.2 Eq. 6.4 predicts it from the other three exponents and reports ≈ 0.054. Appendix B.2 Eq. B.7 writes the identical formula and reports ≈ 0.052.

Do the arithmetic with Table 5's own numbers: 1/0.76 + 1/0.21 + 1/0.076 = 19.24, so the reciprocal is 0.0520. B.7 reproduces; §6.2 does not. And §6.2 calls 0.054 excellent agreement with Fig. 13, which plots −0.050. Quote 0.050 as the fit and 0.052 as the prediction; treat 0.054 as an error in the paper.

⚠️ Trap — the text and the figures disagree on the compute constants

Eq. 1.3 and Table 5 say Ccmin = 3.1e8 PF-days. Figures 1 and 13 both plot L = (Cmin/2.3e8)−0.050. Same for the naive law: Table 5 says Cc = 1.6e7, Fig. 13 plots 2.0e7.

This is specific to the compute laws. The N and D constants agree with their figures exactly — Fig. 1 plots 8.8e13 and 5.4e13. If you are quoting a scale constant for compute, say which one you are reading off.

The two joint forms. Overfitting first (Eq. 1.5), fitted with 10% dropout and early stopping:

L(N, D) = [ (Nc/N)αN/αD + Dc/D ]αD

Chosen from three stated principles: it must rescale cleanly under a vocabulary change, it must limit correctly to L(D) as N → ∞ and to L(N) as D → ∞, and it must be analytic at D = ∞ so it expands in integer powers of 1/D. The paper flags the third as speculative and says its theoretical support is much weaker than the first two — that asymmetry is the only reason N and D enter differently.

Then the learning curve (Eq. 1.6), fitted in the infinite-data limit:

L(N, Smin) = (Nc/N)αN + (Sc/Smin)αS
⚠️ Trap — αD is 0.095 or 0.103, and N0.74 needs the second one

Three fits, three parameter sets. Standalone L(D): αD = 0.095, Dc = 5.4e13. Joint L(N,D), Table 2: αN = 0.076, αD = 0.103, Nc = 6.4e13, Dc = 1.8e13. Joint L(N,S), Table 3: αN = 0.077, αS = 0.76, Nc = 6.5e13, Sc = 2.1e3. The paper acknowledges the drift and attributes it to fitting the full surface rather than the two edges.

Now the load-bearing part. §1.2 says Eqs. 1.1 and 1.2 together give D ∝ NαN/αD ~ N0.74. They do not: 0.076/0.095 = 0.80. The 0.74 comes from Table 2's joint fit, 0.076/0.103 = 0.738. That exponent then propagates into the overfitting threshold and into the §6.3 breakdown argument, so it is worth knowing which fit it came from.

The overfitting threshold itself (Eq. 4.4), derived from an estimated seed-to-seed loss variation of 0.02:

D ≥ (5e3) · N0.74

Read as a rule: an 8× increase in model size needs only about 5× the data to avoid a penalty, since 80.74 = 4.7. Consequence stated in §4.2 — models below 1e9 parameters train on the 22B-token WebText2 with minimal overfitting; the largest ones show mild overfitting.

⚠️ Trap — run-to-run noise is 0.02 in one place and 0.05 in another

§4.2 estimates seed variation at 0.02, and Eq. 4.4's threshold is derived from that figure. The Fig. 22 caption puts run-to-run variation at 0.05 and says averaging multiple runs is needed to validate changes below that. If 0.05 is the right number, Eq. 4.4's coefficient is optimistic by a factor you can work out from the exponent.

⚠️ Trap — "5.5× and 1.8×" is not Kaplan's phrasing

Kaplan's own version, in the Fig. 14 caption, is 5× model size per 10× compute, with data examples processed growing by only 2×. Same exponents underneath — Table 6 gives pN = 0.73, pD = 0.27, and 100.73 = 5.37, 100.27 = 1.86. The 5.5/1.8 numbers come from exponentiating; the 5/2 numbers are what the paper prints. Use either, but do not present 5.5/1.8 as a Kaplan quotation.

The exponent has a second version too. Table 6 says pD = 0.27; Eq. 6.7 writes the single-epoch data requirement as (4e10 tokens) · (Cmin/PF-day)0.26. The coefficient doubling is explained — Eq. 6.7 trains at C = 2Cmin — the exponent difference is not.

Chinchilla also records the conclusion it agrees with: large models should not be trained to their lowest possible loss to be compute-optimal. Kaplan puts a number on how far short to stop — Appendix B.2 derives that compute-efficient training should end at αN/αS ≈ 10% above the converged loss. Against a conventional 2% convergence factor, B.3 prices the difference: 2.7× more parameters, 7.7× fewer parameter updates, 65% less compute for the same loss. The disagreement with Chinchilla is only about the N/D split.

Why being wrong about model size is cheap

Fig. 12 and Appendix B.4: models between 0.6× and 2.2× the optimal size reach the same loss on a 20% larger compute budget, and a 2.2× oversized model gets there in 45% fewer steps for that 20%. This is the flat-optimum result that everything downstream — inference-aware training, over-training past Chinchilla ratios — exploits. Kaplan's own caveat: the equation only holds in the power-law region of the learning curve and should not be trusted for very large models.

The contradiction Kaplan flags himself — §6.3

Controlling overfitting wants D ∝ N0.74 ∝ Cmin0.54. Single-epoch compute-efficient training only supplies D ∝ Cmin0.26. So the compute law, falling as C−0.050, must eventually cross the data-limited floor falling as C−0.03. Intersection (Eq. 6.8): C* ~ 1e4 PF-days, N* ~ 1e12 params, D* ~ 1e12 tokens, L* ~ 1.7 nats/token.

Read the caveat in the same sentence before quoting it: those values are highly uncertain and vary by an order of magnitude in either direction. The paper's own reading is that the laws break down at or before that point; the conjecture that L* estimates the entropy of natural language is offered as a speculation, not a result.

The FLOP identity everyone quotes

C ≈ 6ND

Derive it live, because interviewers ask: a multiply-accumulate is 2 FLOPs, so the forward pass costs about 2N FLOPs per token. The backward pass costs roughly twice the forward (gradients w.r.t. both inputs and weights). Total 2N + 4N = 6N per token, times D tokens.

That is Kaplan's own derivation, not a reconstruction. §2.1 attributes the factor of two to the multiply-accumulate, writes the forward cost as Cforward ≈ 2N + 2 nlayer nctx dmodel, then applies a backward pass of approximately twice the forward to land on 6N per training token.

Two differences from the way it is usually quoted. Kaplan writes the budget as C ≈ 6NBS — batch size times steps — and only introduces D = B · S separately in Eq. 1.7. And his C is explicitly non-embedding compute over an N that excludes both the nvocabdmodel embedding matrix and the nctxdmodel positional parameters, using N ≈ 12 nlayer dmodel2 under dattn = dff/4 = dmodel. Chinchilla's N includes both. Same formula, two different Ns.

The empirical justification, from Chinchilla

Chinchilla computed FLOPs exactly instead — including embeddings, which it also counts in N — and assumed the same 2× backward factor. Their ratio of exact FLOPs to 6ND across six model sizes (Table A4): 1.03, 1.10, 1.08, 1.04, 1.03, 0.99. So the approximation is good to about 10%, and it gets better with scale as the embedding contribution shrinks. Quoting the range as evidence beats asserting that 6ND is fine.

The gap is not free, though. Chinchilla's more accurate count puts the Gopher budget at 6.3e23 FLOPs rather than the 5.76e23 quoted from the Gopher paper — a 9% difference on the single number the whole Chinchilla projection hangs on.

Why Kaplan drops embeddings, with the evidence

Fig. 6 is the whole argument in one plot. Count embeddings in N and performance appears to depend on depth as well as parameter count; exclude them and every model with 2 or more layers collapses onto a single trend. §3.2 draws the practical inference — that the embedding matrix can be shrunk without hurting performance. It is also why Kaplan can quote a smallest model of 768 non-embedding parameters, a number that would be meaningless with a 50257-token vocabulary counted in.

What 6ND leaves out

Attention is not in it. The sequence-dependent terms live outside N, and you can derive their size directly from Chinchilla's Appendix F, which lists the forward pass term by term. Per layer, the terms that scale with sequence length s are:

   Q@K logits         2 * s * s * (k * h)
   softmax            3 * h * s * s
   softmax @ V        2 * s * s * (k * h)
   ------------------------------------------
   with d = k * h:    4 s^2 d  +  3 h s^2   ->  ~4 s^2 d per layer

   per token:         4 s d                    (divide by s)
   forward+backward:  x3
   all layers:        x L
   ------------------------------------------
   attention FLOPs per token  ~  12 * L * d * s
⚠️ The regime where 6ND stops being safe

Negligible at short context, dominant at long: the attention term grows linearly in s while 6N does not grow at all. That is exactly why long-context training economics diverge from the naive scaling picture, and where your FlashAttention notes connect.

⚠️ Two crossover points, and Kaplan's is the looser one

Kaplan states his own condition: the context-dependent cost is a small fraction of the total for models with dmodel > nctx/12, and Appendix C warns the compute scalings may be confounded once nctx reaches roughly 12 dmodel. It follows from his Table 1, which charges exactly one sequence-dependent forward term, 2 nlayer nctx dattn per token. Against 2N = 24 nlayer dmodel2 that ratio is s/(12d), which is 1 at s = 12 d.

The Chinchilla Appendix F accounting above is about 2× larger, because it charges the QK product and the ×V reduction separately and adds softmax: 4sd per layer per token forward. Against 6N = 72 nlayer d2 per token the ratio is s/(6d), so attention matches the parameter term at nctx ≈ 6 dmodel — half Kaplan's threshold. Quote 12× as Kaplan's stated condition and 6× as what the fuller accounting gives, and do not attribute either to the other paper.

⚠️ Trap — Kaplan has two FLOP conventions and labels both

Table 1 is headed as a forward-pass count and totals Cforward ≈ 2N + 2 nlayer nctx dattn per token. The training figure is 6N per token, with the 3× forward-plus-backward factor stated in §2.1. So "Kaplan counts the forward pass only" is wrong as a description of his compute budget, and right as a description of his per-token FLOPs table — which is the convention Switch reports in. Minor bonus inconsistency: Eq. 2.2 writes that same context term with dmodel where Table 1 writes dattn. They are equal under his stated convention, so nothing breaks, but the two forms are not interchangeable in general.

Batch size is where the analysis gets its second layer. Eq. 5.1 defines a tradeoff curve between steps and examples, (S/Smin − 1)(E/Emin − 1) = 1, and the critical batch size is the ratio Bcrit = Emin/Smin. Training at Bcrit costs 2×Smin steps and 2×Emin examples. The result that makes it usable: Bcrit depends on the loss only, not directly on model size, and doubles for roughly every 13% decrease in loss. At convergence for the largest models it is 1–2 million tokens.

What it got wrong, and why

Kaplan used a fixed cosine learning-rate schedule set to a large token count for all runs, then read intermediate losses off those curves to estimate performance at smaller D. Chinchilla §2 names 130B tokens as the schedule length in question.

Kaplan's own setup corroborates the 130B figure

§2.2: every run trains a fixed 2.5e5 steps at 512 sequences of 1024 tokens. That is 219 = 524,288 tokens per step, so the schedule horizon is 2.5e5 × 5.24e5 = 1.31e11 tokens — 131 billion, which is the 130B Chinchilla names. You can state this from the primary source without citing Chinchilla at all.

And §3.3 confirms the mechanism: for each compute value the frontier is built by scanning over model sizes for the best loss at the corresponding step, i.e. by reading an intermediate point off a curve calibrated for 131B tokens.

The mechanism

Loss is minimised only when the schedule length matches the actual token count. An intermediate point on a schedule calibrated for 130B tokens is therefore an overestimate of the loss a model would reach if properly trained to that point. That systematically understates the value of training on fewer tokens — which biases the fit toward "grow N faster than D."

Chinchilla quantifies the underlying effect in Appendix B: overshooting the intended number of steps by more than 25% visibly degrades final loss. Kaplan's intermediate points overshoot by far more than that.

⚠️ Kaplan explicitly denies this is a problem

§2.2 reports that results at convergence were largely independent of the schedule, and §D.6 concludes the choice of schedule is mostly irrelevant provided the summed learning rate is large enough and there is a warmup plus a final decay to near zero. Both statements are about runs taken to convergence. Chinchilla's objection is about points read off mid-curve, which those experiments never test. If you are asked whether Kaplan should have caught it, that is the answer — the control was run on the one regime where the artefact does not appear.

Which of Kaplan's fits actually inherit the artefact

Not all of them, and saying so is a cheap way to sound like you read it. The L(D) trend of §3.3 came from genuinely training one (36, 1280) model on fixed subsets of WebText2 and stopping when test loss stopped falling — real runs, real early stopping. The compute frontier L(C) and the learning-curve law L(N, Smin) are the ones assembled from intermediate points, and the allocation follows from those.

Secondary factor: Chinchilla observes that many of Kaplan's runs were under 100M parameters, while the majority of Chinchilla's own runs exceed 500M, and that there is slight curvature in the FLOP-loss frontier that only appears at larger scale. Chinchilla used models up to 16B.

How to talk about it

Do not dismiss the paper. The power-law framing, the 6ND identity, and the finding that scale beats architecture all survived. Exactly one conclusion — the N/D allocation — was overturned, for one identifiable methodological reason. That is the answer to "why did two careful papers disagree?"

And the disagreement was settled by experiment, not argument: Chinchilla §D.4 trained both predictions head to head at 1e21 FLOPs. See the Chinchilla tab.

What the authors say against themselves

Appendix C is a list of caveats, and it is more candid than the paper's reputation suggests.

  • No theory. They have no theoretical understanding of any of the proposed laws, and call the scaling with model size and compute especially mysterious. Their own conclusion: without a theory or a systematic account of the corrections, it is hard to say when the laws can be trusted.
  • Bcrit(L) does not extrapolate. They are not confident in it outside the loss range explored, and note that changes there would move the parallelism-vs-serial-steps tradeoff substantially.
  • The small-data regime is untested. The L(N,D) fits are poor at the smallest D, where an epoch was only 40 parameter updates, and they ran no regularisation or data-augmentation sweep.
  • Long context is out of scope. C ≈ 6NBS omits the nctx terms, so the compute scalings may be confounded once nctx is large relative to dmodel.
  • Hyperparameters. They may have failed to tune something that matters for scaling, and name initialisation scale and momentum. They also did not try higher learning rates on runs stopped short of convergence — exactly the runs the allocation result depends on.

Three more from the body, not the caveats list. §3.3 concedes that because batch size was held fixed across models, those empirical results are not truly optimal — which is what the whole Cmin correction exists to repair. §6.3 states the trends must eventually level off because natural language has non-zero entropy. And §D.7 records what was excluded from the fits: single-layer models from both L(C) and L(N), and the largest not-fully-converged models from L(N).

Which of Kaplan's exponents would you quote, and which would you refuse to?

Quote αN = 0.076 without qualification — it is stable across all three fits (0.076, 0.076, 0.077). Quote αCmin = 0.050 and say it is the batch-corrected one, since the fixed-batch fit gives 0.057 and the paper's own footnote says to use Cmin.

Refuse to give a single αD. It is 0.095 fitting the data edge alone and 0.103 in the joint L(N,D) fit, and which one you need depends on the question: 0.095 for the pure data law, 0.103 if you are reproducing the N0.74 overfitting relation, which does not come out of 0.095.

Refuse 0.054 outright. It appears in §6.2 as a prediction from the other three exponents, but the same formula in Appendix B.7 gives 0.052, and 0.052 is what Table 5's numbers actually produce.

Kaplan says the learning-rate schedule barely matters. Chinchilla says it is the entire problem. Who is right?

Both, about different things. Kaplan's evidence (§2.2, §D.6) is that converged loss is largely insensitive to the schedule shape, given warmup and a decay to near zero. That is true and is not what Chinchilla disputes.

Chinchilla's objection is about unconverged points. Every Kaplan run is a fixed 2.5e5 steps at 219 tokens per step — a 131B-token horizon — and the compute frontier is assembled by reading losses off those curves at intermediate steps. A cosine sized for 131B tokens has not finished decaying at step 20k, so the loss there is higher than a model properly trained to 20k steps would reach. That inflates the apparent cost of small-D training and tilts the fit toward large N.

The reason Kaplan did not catch it is that the control was run in the converged regime, where the artefact is invisible.

Where does N0.74 come from, and why does it matter that it is not 0.80?

§1.2 introduces it as αN/αD from Eqs. 1.1 and 1.2, but those give 0.076/0.095 = 0.80. The 0.74 is 0.076/0.103, using the αD from Table 2's joint L(N,D) fit.

It matters because 0.74 is not a decoration. It sets the overfitting threshold D ≥ 5e3 · N0.74, it gives the 8×-params-needs-5×-data rule, and it is the input to the §6.3 argument that the laws must break down near 1e12 parameters. Swap in 0.80 and every one of those numbers moves.

The general lesson is the one worth saying out loud: in these papers the joint fit and the marginal fits are different fits, and quoting an exponent without saying which surface it came from is how you get caught.

Different fitting methodologies, different model sets, converging answers. That is why the result was believed immediately and why it displaced Kaplan within months. If you only remember one structural fact about this paper, remember that it triangulated.

The parametric fit

L̂(N, D) = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ

From a classical risk decomposition (§D.2), each term has a meaning:

  • E — Bayes risk. The loss of an ideal generative process; the entropy of natural text.
  • A/Nα — function approximation. A perfectly trained transformer with N params still falls short of the ideal.
  • B/Dβ — stochastic approximation. Finite optimisation steps on a sample, single epoch.

Fitted: E = 1.69, A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28.

Where the two ½s come from — and which one is a surprise

The paper does not say "both exponents should be near ½". It gives a different justification for each, and treats only one of the two shortfalls as notable:

  • α: the 1/N1/2 expectation comes from an approximation-rate result for two-layer networks (Siegel & Xu 2020). Transformers are not that, so the extrapolation is loose to begin with.
  • β: single-epoch training is early stopping in a stochastic first-order method, whose convergence rate is lower-bounded by 1/D1/2. The paper says a data coefficient below ½ is expected — and "far from the known lower-bound".

Their actual closing remark: future models and training approaches should endeavour to increase these coefficients.

Fitting details

Huber loss with δ = 1e-3 on log-loss, minimised with L-BFGS in a log-sum-exp parameterisation (A, B, E = exp(a), exp(b), exp(e)), best fit taken from a grid of initialisations: α, β ∈ {0, 0.5, …, 2}, e ∈ {−1, −0.5, …, 1}, a, b ∈ {0, 5, …, 25}. They note the optimum is not on the boundary of that sweep. Larger δ overfits the low-compute regime and predicts held-out large runs badly; smaller δ changes nothing.

Nopt = G(C/6)a,   Dopt = G−1(C/6)b,   G = (αA/βB)1/(α+β),   a = β/(α+β),   b = α/(α+β)

Note the constraint used to derive this is FLOPs(N,D) ≈ 6ND, cited to Kaplan — the approximation the paper elsewhere shows is only good to 10%.

⚠️ Why Approach 3 disagrees with 1 and 2

Low-FLOP points (C ≤ 1e21) have larger residuals, so the Huber loss automatically treats them as outliers and upweights high-FLOP points. Combined with the observed negative curvature in the C → Nopt frontier (Appendix E), that pushes Approach 3 to predict smaller optimal models — and the gap widens with budget. This is the honest answer to "which exponent do you believe."

⚠️ Two things wrong with the numbers as published
  • The intervals are too narrow to be real. Approach 3's bootstrapped range for a is (0.454, 0.455) — three significant figures of agreement from a five-parameter fit. Besiroglu et al. ⚠️ The caveat the paper raises about its own results

Chinchilla saw 4× more data, so train/test leakage may inflate the pure LM benchmarks. The paper explicitly says to weight MMLU and BIG-bench more heavily than Wikitext/Pile numbers. Citing this unprompted signals you read past the abstract.

Limitations, stated in the paper

  • Only two comparable large runs (Chinchilla and Gopher). No intermediate-scale validation.
  • Power law assumed, but concavity is observed in log Nopt at high budgets (Appendix E) — so optimal size may still be overestimated. They fit the first, middle and final thirds of the frontier separately and get different slopes.
  • All analysis runs are under one epoch; the multi-epoch regime is left to future work. (Chinchilla's own final mix is not uniform: Wikipedia 3.4 epochs, MassiveWeb 1.24, Books 0.75, C4 0.77, News 0.21, GitHub 0.13.)
  • Projections get steep fast: a 1T-param model needs 1.27e26 FLOPs, over 250× Gopher.
  • Not stated as a limitation but worth pairing with the above: the 6ND constraint used to derive the frontier is itself accurate only to ~10%, and their exact count moves the Gopher budget to 6.3e23.

The conclusion's own forward pointer: dataset scaling deserves more focus, and scaling to larger datasets is only beneficial when the data is high quality — which is the setup for tab 05.

10× more compute — what changes?

About 3.2× params and 3.2× tokens (100.5). Contrast with Kaplan: 5.5× params, 1.8× tokens.

Which of the three exponents do you actually believe?

1 and 2 agree closely and use different methods, so 0.50 is the robust answer. Approach 3's 0.46 is not wrong so much as differently weighted: its Huber loss downweights the C ≤ 1e21 points as outliers, and the frontier has negative curvature, so it leans smaller — increasingly so at large budgets.

Then add the caveat: Approach 3's bootstrap interval is (0.454, 0.455), which is implausibly tight for a five-parameter fit on this much data, and Besiroglu's replication attempt says exactly that. For practical purposes a ≈ b ≈ 0.5, and the paper's own concavity observation suggests the true answer drifts below 0.5 at frontier scale.

Where does "20 tokens per parameter" come from?

Not from the text. Divide Table 3's token column by its parameter column: 20.0, 20.2, 20.5, 22.4, then ~21 for everything larger. The paper's own claim is the scale-invariant one — double the model, double the tokens — and the ratio is a consequence of it at the budgets they projected.

Directly, in §D.4. At 1e21 FLOPs Kaplan's fit predicts a 4.68B optimum and Approach 1 predicts 2.86B; they trained 4.74B and 2.80B models at matched depth-to-width, same batch size and LR schedule. When would you deliberately violate Chinchilla?

Inference-heavy serving (train smaller, longer — see next tab); data-constrained regimes; distillation targets; latency or memory SLAs that cap model size; and any case where the deployment horizon is long relative to training.

The gap Chinchilla leaves

Chinchilla minimises training compute for a target loss. But a deployed model is paid for twice: once to train, then continuously to serve. Demand for popular models runs to billions of tokens per day.

   TOTAL COST = training + inference

   training  = 6 N D_train                    <- one time
   inference = 2 N D_inference                <- forward only, forever

   Chinchilla minimises term 1.
   Minimising the SUM moves the optimum to smaller N, larger D.

Both FLOP counts are the standard transformer approximations, cited to Kaplan: 6N per training token, 2N per inference token, forward-only.

The converse problem

N*, D*tr = argminN,Dtr | L(N,Dtr)=ℓ   6NDtr + 2NDinf
The structural insight — say this one out loud

This is the converse of the Chinchilla optimisation, and the paper says so explicitly. Chinchilla fixes compute and minimises loss. This paper fixes loss and minimises compute. Same constraint surface, objective and constraint swapped.

Everything else follows. Once quality is the constraint rather than the objective, "how much will I serve this?" becomes a term in the thing you are minimising, and the answer moves.

The constraint is Chinchilla's third scaling law, L(N, Dtr) = E + A/Nα + B/Dtrβ, with Chinchilla's fitted coefficients.

⚠️ There is no closed form — and they prove it

Appendix A, Theorem A.1: with Dinf = 0 the problem solves analytically, but for Dinf > 0 it does not. Lagrange multipliers reduce it to finding roots of ax−1.283 + 756.6x−0.283 + c = 0, which is not solvable in general — only for special-case values of β that make it a low-degree polynomial. They solve it numerically with Newton root-finding instead.

Two things fall out of that proof that are worth carrying: the exponents in it are β = 0.283 and β+1 = 1.283, not the published 0.28 — and their footnote says plainly that Chinchilla's printed α = 0.34, β = 0.28 are rounded, and that α = 0.336, β = 0.283 are needed to reproduce Chinchilla's own Table A3.

One assumption to be able to name: conditioned on pre-training loss, inference demand is taken to be independent of model size and token count — models of equal quality see the same requests. Their own footnote concedes this is optimistic, since smaller models have lower latency and may therefore attract more demand.

When inference volume is well below the pre-training token count, Chinchilla models are essentially compute-optimal. The framing to use

Chinchilla answers "what should I train?" in the currency of FLOPs. This paper answers "what should I deploy?" in the currency of total lifetime cost — and then shows that even FLOPs is the wrong currency, because generation runs at 1% MFU. Both are correct given their objective; the interview question is usually about noticing which objective applies.

Isn't this just Chinchilla with an extra term?

No — it is Chinchilla's objective and constraint swapped. Chinchilla fixes compute and minimises loss; this fixes loss and minimises compute. That swap is why it has no analytic solution (Theorem A.1) and needs Newton root-finding, while Chinchilla's does close.

The second difference is that it does not stop at FLOPs. The FLOP-optimal answer saves 2.6–16%; the cost-optimal answer saves 34–58%, because a generated token runs at ~1% MFU against ~50% for training.

Is there a floor? Can a small model always catch up given enough tokens?

They found no floor out to 10,000 tokens per parameter on a 150M model — no plateau in loss, and no plateau in downstream Gauntlet accuracy either. That is direct evidence against De Vries' critical-model-size hypothesis (~30% of Chinchilla size as a hard floor), though they are careful to say behaviour past 10,000 is untested.

The caveat that matters more: their §5 refit shows loss falls out there more slowly than the Chinchilla law predicts. So "no floor" and "cheap" are different claims, and only the first is supported.

Should I use Chinchilla's fitted coefficients for an over-trained model?

Not without knowing which way the error runs. Chinchilla's coefficients were fitted almost entirely on runs at ≤~100 tokens/param, and refitting on data out to 10,000 makes the curves progressively flatter (α 0.08→0.18, β 0.13→0.24 across their subsets). Used at extreme ratios, the original law overestimates what more data buys.

And use the unrounded values, α = 0.336, β = 0.283 — the printed 0.34 and 0.28 do not reproduce Chinchilla's own Table A3.

The question

Chinchilla assumes effectively unlimited data — its parametric fit is built on the assumption of a single epoch, and this paper says so directly in §5: because Chinchilla never repeats a whole corpus and its fit relies on one-epoch training, there is no guarantee its predictions survive into the repeated-data regime. But Dopt ∝ C0.54 grows fast, and high-quality text is finite. What happens when you must repeat data?

The framing to borrow, from §2: every scaling question is either Allocation (what is the best split of a fixed budget?) or Return (what is another unit of budget worth?). The paper answers both separately, and they have different answers under a data constraint.

The numbers that motivate it, all from §1: extrapolating Chinchilla to a 530B model implies 11 trillion tokens, more than 30 terabytes of text, against MT-NLG's actual 270B. Villalobos et al. put the exhaustion of high-quality English text at 2024. And for most languages the ceiling is orders of magnitude lower — the 100M-token budget swept here is chosen as a realistic constraint for Basque, Punjabi or Slovenian after filtering OSCAR.

Findings

  • Up to 4 epochs is nearly free. The abstract's claim is that up to 4 epochs of repeated data changes loss negligibly against unique data. The measurement behind it (§6): an 8.7B model trained 4 epochs on a 44B-token unique budget finishes 0.5% higher in validation loss than the same-FLOP single-epoch run with 178B unique tokens. Downstream (§7), task differences are insignificant to about 4 epochs — a 25% data budget — and start dropping after.
  • Returns die at about 16 epochs, not 4. The fitted decay constant is R*D = 15.4 repetitions, i.e. 16 epochs, at which point repeated tokens have lost 1/e of their value. §6's summary: meaningful gains are available to roughly 16 epochs, and past that returns collapse fast.
  • There is a hard ceiling, and it is the most useful number here. No amount of repetition beats a single epoch on UD(1 + R*D) = 16.4× your unique token count. Infinite compute does not buy past it.
  • Allocation inverts Chinchilla: scale epochs faster than parameters. Because R*N = 5.31 is well below R*D = 15.39, excess parameters decay in value faster than repeated tokens. Under a data constraint the efficient frontier puts most additional compute into epochs, not parameters — against Chinchilla's equal scaling.
  • One-epoch models leave a lot on the table. At a 100M-token budget, the best loss found sits at roughly 20–60× the compute-optimal parameters and epochs, about 7000× more FLOPs, for more than a 50% loss reduction. Compute-inefficient, but available.

The old text read: "spending compute on more params rather than more repeated epochs becomes preferable past that threshold." The paper finds the reverse, and it is the central result of §5. Repeated tokens hold their value roughly 3× longer than excess parameters do (15.39 against 5.31), so under a data constraint you buy epochs before parameters.

Appendix A gives the mechanism in one line: once repetition reaches about R*N, an added parameter is worth only e−R/R*N of an added token despite costing the same FLOPs, so the optimal curve breaks away from Chinchilla proportionality — and it breaks toward data because R*N is reached first.

The confirmation at scale: at 9.3e21 FLOPs with a 25B-token unique budget, the data-constrained optimum is 6.3B parameters for 9.7 epochs, which beats the Chinchilla-shaped model on both loss and downstream score with 27% fewer parameters.

4 epochs is where the penalty is still too small to measure. 16 epochs is where returns collapse. 59 epochs was the empirical optimum at a 100M-token budget (Appendix E, and unchanged by deduplication). None of them is "the optimal number of epochs" in general, and "4 epochs is optimal" is wrong in both directions — it understates the optimum and overstates the point of no return.

The decay law

Start from the Chinchilla parametric form, reproduced as their Eq. 2:

L(N, D) = A/Nα + B/Dβ + E

Split the data term. Given a unique-token budget DC, let UD = min{DC, D} be the unique tokens used and RD = D/UD − 1 the number of repetitions — epochs minus one. Replace D with an effective data term:

D′ = UD + UD R*D (1 − e−RD/R*D)

The derivation is three lines. Assume each pass over a token retains a fraction (1 − δ) of what the previous pass was worth; sum the geometric series over RD repeats; define R*D = (1 − δ)/δ and, for small δ, approximate (1 − δ) ≈ e−1/R*D. Parameters get the symmetric treatment, with UN = min{Nopt(UD), N} and RN = max{N/UN − 1, 0}.

Three limits worth being able to state: at RD = 0 it reduces exactly to Chinchilla; for RD well below R*D repeated tokens are worth almost the same as fresh; as RD → ∞ the effective data plateaus at UD(1 + R*D). Setting R*N = R*D = ∞ recovers the unmodified Chinchilla equation, so it is a drop-in replacement.

⚠️ Trap — "half-life" is the paper's word and it is the wrong word

§6 calls R*D roughly the half-life of epochs, then defines it as the point where repeated tokens have lost 1/e of their value. At RD = R*D the repeated tokens are worth 1 − 1/e ≈ 63% of fresh ones, not 50%. Quote the 1/e definition; the word "half-life" is loose and someone will check it.

The approximation has a stated cost too. Appendix A works an example at δ = 0.25 where the exact series gives 3.05 effective units and the exponential form gives 3.21 — but because the term is then raised to β = 0.353, the difference at the level of loss is 1.8%. Minor bookkeeping: the exponent is written 0.3526596 in Appendix B, 0.353 in Eq. 20 and 0.35 in Eq. 17.

Four deliberate departures from the earlier papers, each of which changes what the numbers mean. No early stopping, so overfitting is visible rather than hidden. The entire corpus is repeated rather than up-sampling a subset, and data is reshuffled each epoch. Runs with fewer unique tokens always use a strict subset of runs with more, so the comparison is clean. And loss is reported on a held-out test set, not the training loss Chinchilla fitted — Appendix H shows why that matters: under repetition, training loss keeps falling as the model memorises, so it stops being a proxy for anything.

The 54-run claim from Sardana et al. §5, checked

Sardana et al. describe this paper as having trained 54 runs on C4 and recovered coefficients similar to Chinchilla's. The count is right; the attribution is not. Appendix B fits on 54 samples, but they are Chinchilla's own C4 data points, refitted here because Chinchilla published a = b = 0.5 for C4 without releasing the coefficients. Muennighoff trained none of those 54.

"Similar coefficients" also overstates it, because α and β were tied equal before fitting — that is exactly what a = b = 0.5 encodes, so agreement on that point is assumed, not found. The real check is the projection: at the Gopher budget of 5.76e23 FLOPs this fit predicts 70.0B params / 1.37T tokens, against the 73B / 1.3T Chinchilla's isoFLOP curves give for C4. That is a genuine agreement and it is what makes the refit usable.

Sardana's underlying point survives intact, and gains force: those 54 points sit at Chinchilla-like token ratios, which is the regime where Sardana et al. show the fitted law overestimates the value of extra data. Two fits agreeing on the same narrow slice does not make the extrapolation safe. The 182 data-constrained runs do reach far past that slice, but they are a different fit with α and β frozen at the values these 54 produced.

One number worth carrying to the Chinchilla tab: for the same Gopher budget, Chinchilla's parametric fit on MassiveWeb predicts 40B parameters against 73B on C4 — nearly a factor of two, from the corpus alone. "20 tokens per parameter" is a property of a dataset, not of transformers.

Why this matters now

Chinchilla itself is not single-epoch in practice — its Wikipedia subset ran 3.4 epochs and MassiveWeb 1.24. And Chinchilla's conclusion explicitly calls for increased focus on dataset scaling, adding that scaling to larger datasets is only beneficial when the data is high quality. This paper is the direct follow-up to that call.

When repeating runs out

§7 tests what else buys tokens, at N = 4.2B and D = 84B total with a maximum unique budget of 84B.

  • Code augmentation. Python from The Stack, shuffled into the natural-language data at 10 mixing rates. Filling up to 50% of the budget with code — 42B tokens — costs nothing on natural-language tasks; past that it drops quickly. Headline: code gives a 2× increase in effective tokens even when only natural-language tasks are scored.
  • Filtering. Start from roughly twice the budget (178B tokens), on the argument that noisy data is cheaper to gather than clean. Perplexity filtering keeps the lowest-perplexity 25% by a Wikipedia-trained KenLM — 44B tokens, repeated about 2 epochs. Deduplication drops any 100-character overlap — 21B tokens, repeated 4 epochs.
  • Result: perplexity filtering helps, deduplication does not on C4, and both are more useful on the noisier OSCAR. The recommendation as stated: reserve filtering for noisy datasets.
  • Stacking them: double the corpus with code, then repeat the result for four epochs, and you have 8× the training tokens at roughly the quality of 8× the unique data.
The bAbI tell

Two of the 19 tasks jump the moment code is added: WebNLG, a generation task, and bAbI, a reasoning task. Models trained on C4 alone score 0.0 on bAbI; OSCAR models do not, and the paper's explanation is that OSCAR contains code while C4 explicitly filtered it out. The proposed mechanism is long-range state tracking. If you are asked why pretraining mixes in code for a text model, this is the cleanest published data point — and note it is an association across corpora, not a controlled ablation of the mechanism.

What the authors say against themselves

  • The law cannot represent harm. Excess epochs and excess parameters empirically hurt — loss decreases then rises again — but Eq. 14 can only plateau. §5 states this outright. Appendix F builds the alternative that can represent harm, by decaying α and β instead, and reports it fits worse (R² = 0.5110) and mispredicts Return. Their defence is that harm is trivially avoidable by stopping training or dropping parameters.
  • Failing runs are underestimated. §6 concedes the fit significantly underestimates final loss for models whose loss rises mid-training, such as the 44-epoch runs.
  • Double descent contaminates the fit. Appendix D finds epoch-wise double descent — loss rising around 200 epochs then falling again — and most such runs were removed from the fit because the functional form assumes monotonic improvement.
  • Hyperparameters are unswept. Appendix Q: returns from extra epochs may depend heavily on learning rate, dropout and optimiser, and they expect a higher learning rate would bring diminishing returns forward. Everything was fixed at conventional values. Appendix F adds that muP gave higher test loss than their defaults and did not stop excess parameters hurting.
  • Partial repetition is out of scope. They always repeat the whole corpus. Up-sampling a subset — Hernandez et al. found repeating 0.1% of data 100 times degrades performance badly — would need extra parameters in the law, possibly including when in training the repeat happens.
  • Two corpora, one architecture. C4 and OSCAR, GPT-2 only; they decline to give universal filtering advice.
The answer to "we are out of data"

Repeat, and repeat before you add parameters — 4 epochs costs nothing measurable, 16 is where it stops paying, and no amount of repetition beats one epoch on 16.4× your unique tokens. Then go get tokens you do not have: code doubles the effective budget, perplexity filtering pays on noisy corpora, deduplication did not pay on C4.

Under a data constraint, do you buy more epochs or more parameters?

Epochs, and the constants say by how much. The fitted decay constants are R*D = 15.39 for repeated tokens and R*N = 5.31 for excess parameters, so a parameter beyond the compute-optimal count loses its value roughly 3× faster than a repeated token does, at identical FLOP cost. The efficient frontier therefore breaks away from Chinchilla proportionality toward data.

The scale check: at 9.3e21 FLOPs with 25B unique tokens, the data-constrained optimum is 6.3B parameters for 9.7 epochs, and it beats the Chinchilla-shaped 8.7B model on the same unique budget with 27% fewer parameters. Galactica is the counterexample in the wild — 120B parameters on 106B unique tokens for 4.25 epochs, where this law says 40B parameters for 12.75 epochs would have been the better trade.

Say the caveat too: the paper's own fit cannot express excess parameters actively hurting, only plateauing, and Fig. 11 shows they do hurt.

Is "four epochs is free" a real result or a rounding of something else?

Real, and narrow. It means the loss penalty is too small to matter, not that four is optimal. The measurement is one comparison: 8.7B parameters, 4 epochs on 44B unique tokens, finishing 0.5% above the same-FLOP single-epoch run on 178B unique tokens. Downstream scores agree — insignificant differences to about 4 epochs, then decline.

What it is not: the optimum. At a 100M-token budget the best loss was at 59 epochs, and deduplicating the corpus did not move that. And it is not the point of no return either — that is R*D, about 16 epochs. Three numbers, three questions, and conflating them is the trap.

Did this paper independently reproduce Chinchilla's coefficients?

No, and the distinction is worth being precise about because the claim circulates. Appendix B refits the Chinchilla parametric form on 54 samples that are Chinchilla's own C4 data points — not new training runs — because Chinchilla published only a = b = 0.5 for C4 and not the coefficients. And α and β were tied equal before fitting, which is what a = b = 0.5 means, so that part of the agreement is an input.

The non-trivial agreement is downstream: at the Gopher budget the refit predicts 70.0B parameters and 1.37T tokens against Chinchilla's isoFLOP-on-C4 73B and 1.3T. The right summary is that the refit is a faithful reconstruction of Chinchilla on C4, and that the 182-run data-constrained fit sits on top of it with α and β frozen at those values.

The fourth axis

Prior scaling work varied depth, width, and data. This paper adds: increase parameter count while holding FLOPs per example constant. The stated hypothesis is that parameter count, independent of total computation performed, is a separately important axis on which to scale.

The distributed argument: sparsely activated layers split unique weights across devices, so total model weights grow with device count while per-device memory and compute stay manageable.

⚠️ Attribution — Switch does not argue for the Kaplan allocation, it inherits it

§2 says the benefit of scale was exhaustively studied in Kaplan et al. (2020), and that that work advocates training large models on relatively small amounts of data as the computationally optimal approach. Switch's next sentence is "Heeding these results, we investigate a fourth axis." So the Kaplan-era premise is a starting assumption Switch builds on, not a claim it makes.

The practical consequence is the same either way — the premise is superseded by Chinchilla, so Switch's data-budget framing is historical context and never guidance. But if you say "Switch argues for big models on little data," you have misread its second section. Say "Switch inherits Kaplan's allocation, which Chinchilla later overturned."

The Switch layer — k = 1

Prior MoE routed each token to k ≥ 2 experts. Shazeer et al. (2017) conjectured this was necessary for non-trivial gradients to the router, intuiting that learning to route would not work without the ability to compare at least two experts; Ramachandran & Le (2018) went further and found higher k in lower layers mattered. Switch routes to one.

   token x
     |
     v  router: h(x) = W_r x            -> (n_experts,)
     v  p_i(x) = softmax(h(x))_i
     v  pick argmax -> expert i
     v  y = p_i(x) * Expert_i(x)
                ^
                +--- the gate VALUE multiplies the output.
                     This is what keeps the router differentiable:
                     argmax has no gradient, but p_i(x) does.

Three stated benefits, in the paper's order: router computation is reduced; expert capacity can be at least halved, since each token goes to one place; and the routing implementation is simpler with lower communication cost.

The k=1 answer

You do not need k ≥ 2 for the router to learn. The paper's own note: for both MoE and Switch routing, the gate value p_i(x) in the output equation is what permits differentiability of the router. Gradient reaches the router through the gate scaling the expert output, not through comparing experts. Being able to say this cleanly is the point of reading Switch.

Load balancing

Routing is rich-get-richer: an expert that gets more tokens trains faster, so it attracts more tokens, and the rest collapse to dead weight. The auxiliary loss counteracts this.

Laux = α · N · ∑i=1N fi · Pi

where fi is the fraction of tokens dispatched to expert i and Pi is the fraction of router probability mass allocated to expert i across the batch. Both vectors are wanted at 1/N; the dot product is minimised under uniform routing. f is not differentiable (it comes from argmax), P is — that is what makes the product trainable.

Two details people miss on this loss
  • Why multiply by N. Under uniform routing ∑ fiPi = ∑ (1/N)(1/N) = 1/N, so the N factor makes the minimum value 1 regardless of expert count — the loss stays comparable as you sweep the number of experts.
  • The coefficient is 1e-2. Swept from 1e-1 to 1e-5 in powers of 10; α = 1e-2 balanced load quickly without interfering with the cross-entropy objective. Having a number here is better than saying "a small coefficient."
⚠️ The aux loss fights the LM loss

It is an explicit regularizer pulling routing away from whatever the LM loss wants. Too small and experts collapse; too large and you damage quality — which is exactly the tension the 1e-2 sweep was resolving. DeepSeek-V3's aux-loss-free approach exists to eliminate it; see the DeepSeek tab.

Capacity factor and token dropping

expert capacity = (tokens per batch / number of experts) × capacity factor

Each expert gets a fixed buffer, because TPU tensor shapes are statically declared at compile time while routing is dynamic. Tokens routed to a full expert are dropped — computation is skipped and the representation passes to the next layer through the residual connection.

   capacity_factor = 1.25, 4 experts, 8 tokens
   capacity per expert = (8/4) * 1.25 = 2.5 -> 2

Measured drop rate: typically <1%, with no observed dependence on the number of experts, provided the aux loss coefficient is high enough. Higher capacity factors reduce overflow but waste computation and memory on padding.

Scaling results

Pre-training setup: C4, masked LM objective, 15% of tokens dropped and replaced by a single sentinel, 220 = 1,048,576 tokens per batch for 550k steps = 576B tokens. Fine-tuning at batch 1M for 16k steps, evaluating every 200 steps and reporting peak validation.

  • Scaling experts from 1 to 256 at fixed FLOPs/token improves test loss monotonically — from T5-Base's 223M at the top left to a 256-expert 14.7B model at the bottom right of Figure 4.
  • Step basis: Switch-Base with 64 experts reaches T5-Base's step-60k quality at step 450k — a 7.5× speedup in steps.
  • Wall-clock: 7× over T5-Base on 32 TPUv3 cores at equal FLOPs per example.
  • Against a bigger dense model: 2.5× over T5-Large — despite T5-Large applying 3.5× more FLOPs per token. That comparison is the one that answers "why not just train a bigger dense model?"
  • Multilingual: all 101 languages improve (107 tasks, because of script variants). Mean step speedup over mT5-Base is 5×, and 91% of languages see ≥4×.
Distillation — the deployment answer

Two techniques compound: initialise the dense student's non-expert weights from the teacher (possible because they are FLOP-matched, so shapes align), and train on a 0.75 hard / 0.25 soft mix of ground truth and teacher probabilities. Distilling Switch-Base (3.8B) into T5-Base (223M) recovers 29% of the quality gain, versus 3% for naive distillation and 20% with initialisation alone.

Across teacher sizes the recovered fraction is remarkably flat: 37% at 82% compression (1.1B teacher) down to 28% at 99% compression (14.7B teacher). A fine-tuned SuperGLUE model behaves the same — Switch-Base 7.4B scores 81.3 against T5-Base's 74.6, and the distilled T5-Base gets 76.6, again ~30%.

⚠️ Two counterintuitive results people get backwards
  • The 1.6T model is the stable one. Switch-C, 1571B parameters and 2048 experts, showed no training instability at all. Switch-XXL, at 395B, is sometimes unstable — it applies roughly 10× the FLOPs per token with about 4× fewer unique parameters. Instability tracks FLOPs per token, not parameter count. They stopped Switch-XXL short of 1M steps because of it.
  • Upstream perplexity does not cleanly buy downstream quality. At comparable C4 perplexity, Switch-C scores 87.7 on SQuAD against Switch-XXL's 89.6 — the bigger model losing. The authors call this a poorly understood dependence between fine-tuning quality, FLOPs per token, and parameter count, and leave it open. Appendix E shows the general pattern: for fixed upstream perplexity, dense often wins on reasoning (SuperGLUE) while sparse wins on knowledge (TriviaQA).

Switch-XXL's downstream results, from a checkpoint pre-trained on 503B tokens — roughly half what T5-XXL saw: SQuAD 89.7 (SOTA 91.3), SuperGLUE 87.5 (T5 89.3, SOTA 90.0), ANLI 65.7 against a prior best of 49.4, and closed-book gains on Natural Questions (34.4 vs 32.8), WebQuestions (41.0 vs 37.2) and TriviaQA (47.5 vs 42.9).

No-Token-Left-Behind — a negative result worth knowing

The obvious fix for dropped tokens is to iteratively reroute overflow to each token's next-choice expert, which nearly guarantees nothing is dropped. They built it. It produced no empirical benefit. Their conjecture: once the network has learned token–expert associations, sending a token to its second choice degrades quality by about as much as dropping it. Good answer to "why not just reroute?"

Why does routing to one expert still train the router?

Because the gradient path is the gate value, not the comparison. The layer output is p_i(x) · Expert_i(x); argmax has no gradient but p_i(x) does, so the router receives signal through the scaling factor. The paper states this holds for both top-k MoE and k=1 Switch routing.

The prior belief — Shazeer et al.'s conjecture that k > 1 was needed for non-trivial router gradients — was an intuition, not a proof, and Switch is the counterexample.

Switch's stability fix is not the z-loss. What is it?

Selective precision: cast the router input to float32, keep the float32 arithmetic local to the router body, and recast the dispatch and combine tensors to bfloat16 before any all-to-all — so you get float32 stability without paying float32 communication. Full bfloat16 diverges (−3.780); selective precision matches float32 quality (−1.716 vs −1.718) at full bfloat16 speed (1390 vs 1160 ex/s).

Second fix, independent: reduce the initialisation scale 10×, which cuts seed-to-seed quality variance from 0.68 to 0.01. The router z-loss is ST-MoE, one paper later.

Bigger sparse model, more instability — true?

The opposite, in their data. Switch-C at 1.6T parameters and 2048 experts trained with no instability; Switch-XXL at 395B was sometimes unstable and had to be stopped early. The difference is that Switch-XXL applies ~10× the FLOPs per token with ~4× fewer unique parameters. What destabilises is compute density per token, not parameter count — which is also why their remaining open problem is stability for the highest-FLOP models.

Why sparse models are unstable

The precision argument, which is the most interesting part of the paper and the part that rewards your numerics background:

  • Numbers in any range between consecutive powers of 2 (e.g. [2,4), [1024,2048)) share a fixed number of mantissa bits — 7 for bfloat16, 23 for float32.
  • So bfloat16 carries about 65,536× the roundoff error of float32 (223-7 = 216).
  • Larger numbers have larger roundoff error. With 8 exponent bits values reach ~3e38, so even float32 has issues at the top of the range. Both formats have the same 8 exponent bits; the gap is mantissa only.
  • Sparse models introduce additional exponential functions through the router, which amplifies all of this.

Two concrete failure modes:

  1. Roundoff does not change the ordering within a softmax, but it does change routing of the second token under relative thresholding — a token goes to its second-choice expert only if that gate probability is at least ~1/5 of the first. Noise flips that test.
  2. Roundoff changes the gate value that scales the expert output, which the paper found to be important.

They conjecture decoder-only models were more stable because they have fewer exponential functions. The same reasoning covers two other observations they report: encoder-decoder models are less stable than decoder-only at fixed FLOPs (self-attention and encoder-decoder attention per decoder FFN), and deeper models are less stable than shallower ones at fixed FLOPs. More attention layers, more exponentials.

The derivation — where the damage actually happens

Footnote 7 carries the whole mechanism. Take eleven logits: ten at 128, one at 128.5. Softmax subtracts the max for numerical stability, so the comparison is 0 against ten copies of −0.5. At magnitude 128 the bfloat16 roundoff is large enough to erase the 0.5, collapsing 128.5 to 128 and flattening all eleven.

exact   e0 / (e0 + 10·e−0.5) ≈ 0.142   →   rounded   e0 / (e0 + 10·e0) ≈ 0.091

A 36% shift in the gate from a roundoff of 0.5. No ordering changed; the magnitudes did. The paper notes the analogous situation occurs in float32 at larger logit values — float32 is further along the same curve, not off it.

⚠️ Trap — the 1/5 rule is a simplification of the paper's own algorithm

§3.4 states relative thresholding as a test: the second expert fires only if its gate is 1/5 as large as the first. Appendix G gives the algorithm actually used, and it is stochastic. Normalise the top-n gates so they sum to one, then route to each of the other n−1 experts with probability min(1.0, gatei / threshold), threshold typically 0.2. A normalised gate of 0.1 is not rejected — it is routed half the time. Quote Appendix G and mention §3.4's shorthand, not the reverse. They also tried an absolute rather than relative threshold and report it works as well once tuned.

The router z-loss

Lz = (1/B) ∑i (log ∑j exj(i))2

Penalises large logits entering the exponential by squaring the log-sum-exp. B is the number of tokens, N the number of experts, and x ∈ RB×N the logits going into the router. Not a new invention: the paper presents it as an adaptation of the z-loss already applied to final softmax logits in the Mesh TensorFlow codebase.

Ltot = LCE + cBLB + czLZ   —   cz = 0.001, load-balance α = 10−2

cz = 0.001 was chosen by sweep on post-pre-training quality. Appendix B shows why the sweep matters in one direction: at 1e-2 or larger the z-loss itself collapses to near zero, meaning the penalty has flattened the logits rather than regulating them.

Why z-loss beats logit clipping — the whole argument is ordering

Clipping happens after any roundoff error has already occurred, so it adds an even larger discontinuity on top. In one view, clipping is itself a roundoff error. The z-loss instead encourages the model to produce small logits, which are then accurately representable going into the exp. Prevention versus post-hoc correction.

⚠️ Trap — two different clippings, and only one was measured

The argument above is about clipping the router logits, and the paper refutes it by reasoning only. That experiment is never run. The clipping in the table above that scores −4.206 is Adafactor update clipping at clip = 0.1 — a constraint on weight changes, not on logits. Both stabilise 3/3; only one has a number attached. Asked "didn't they just clip?", separate them: update clipping was tested and was catastrophic for quality, logit clipping was argued against on roundoff grounds. They also note looser clipping values failed to stabilise reliably, so those runs are excluded from the table rather than reported as failures.

Corollary they draw: ensure all exponentiated tensors are cast to float32. A footnote adds that z-losses on the attention logits also reduce instability without hurting quality — which is the lever for dense models, where there is no router. And a speculative note — adding z-losses throughout a network leaves exponent bits unused, hinting at better number formats for neural networks.

Fine-tuning sparse models

  • Fine-tuning MoE is an overfitting problem relative to dense — far more params, same small downstream dataset. The demonstration is two SuperGLUE tasks at opposite sizes: CB (250 train examples) and ReCoRD (138k). Sparse reaches 100% train accuracy faster on both; on ReCoRD it beats dense on held-out data by several points, on CB it loses.
  • Fine-tuning only the non-expert params performs about as well as full fine-tuning, and non-MoE-FFN-only is the best of the five subsets tried (all, non-MoE, MoE, attention, non-MoE FFN).
  • Fine-tuning only the expert params is drastically worse — and that is where ≈80% of the parameters live. Their hypothesis: expert layers occur only every 1/4 layers and a token meets at most two experts per layer, so updating MoE params touches far fewer layers and FLOPs than any other subset.
  • Sparse models are robust to dropped tokens during fine-tuning. At train CF 0.75 with the aux loss on, 10.6% of tokens are dropped and SuperGLUE is 86.5; at CF 1.25 only 0.3% drop and it is 86.7. Turning the aux loss off raises drops to 15.6% and costs about a point. Dropping 10–15% performs roughly as well as dropping under 1%.
  • Sparse models want smaller batches and higher learning rates than dense; dense behaves nearly oppositely. Resetting the optimiser slot variables hurts both.
⚠️ Correction — freezing the experts does not generalise better

The tempting version of this finding is "update fewer params, regularise better, generalise better." The paper says the opposite in as many words: updating all the non-MoE parameters produces a higher training loss than updating everything, and that regularisation effect did not translate into better validation performance. The real case for non-expert-only fine-tuning is speed and memory, not quality. Note also that these five subsets are reported only as a bar chart averaged over 5 seeds — there is no table of values, so quote the ordering, not a number.

Two further fine-tuning results worth having. Sentinel tokens inserted during fine-tuning, to make the task look more like span corruption, help Grammar Error Correction (22.2 → 22.9 exact match) and do nothing for SuperGLUE (86.6 → 86.6); they do accelerate convergence for dense and sparse alike. And a negative result they report honestly: a dropout variant masking out entire experts stochastically failed to improve generalisation.

Design recommendations

  • Top-2 routing, train capacity factor 1.25 and eval capacity factor 2.0, at most one expert per core.
  • Capacity factor can be changed at evaluation time to adapt to different memory/compute budgets — a nice property, since it is a deployment knob that needs no retraining.
  • Dense layer stacking and a multiplicative bias improve quality. An extra FFN immediately before or after each sparse layer is worth +0.014 neg. log perp against +0.007 for the same number of FFNs placed randomly. A multiplicative bias in the expert layer is worth +0.008 and about a 4% convergence speedup at no step-time cost; the additive bias is worth −0.002, i.e. nothing.
  • Below capacity factor 1.0, use Batch Prioritized Routing — drop by global router probability rather than left-to-right. Encoder only: on the decoder it leaks future-token information and the model learns to cheat.
  • More experts stops paying past roughly 256, or equivalently below ~1% of experts activated. The binding constraint is the compute-to-memory ratio: extra experts add no computation but do add parameters to load.
Note — Switch says k=1, ST-MoE says top-2, and the paper says why

Not a difference of framing or deployment orientation. The paper states it revises the earlier recommendation, and attributes the reversal to scale of compute. Switch trained 220M-FLOP-matched models on 50B tokens; ST-MoE trained 1B-FLOP-matched models on 100B tokens, which they call 8×. At that size the speed penalty of top-(n+1) over top-n vanishes, because the router is a smaller fraction of total model computation. §9 offers it as their own cautionary example of small-scale findings reversing.

Size the claim honestly: top-2 beats top-1 by +0.004 neg. log perp at train CF 1.25, against +0.090 for tripling a dense model. About 1/20th. Their own summary is that raising the capacity factor buys more than smarter routing does.

Where Switch's contribution ends and this one begins

Switch's stability work is selective precision (cast the router input to float32, keep it device-local) and a 10× smaller initialisation scale. ST-MoE's is the z-loss and the mantissa analysis above — and the bridge between them is explicit: ST-MoE reports selective precision as insufficient at the largest scales, which is the reason the z-loss exists at all.

Capacity factor 1.25 belongs to Switch. ST-MoE continues to use it and credits Fedus et al. for advocating it; what ST-MoE adds is new evidence that it still holds at 32B. Switch also has its own fine-tuning contribution — expert dropout — which is separate from ST-MoE's non-expert-params-only finding. ST-MoE independently reproduces 0.1 global dropout as the optimum and sweeps expert dropout 0.0–0.5, but names no best value and calls the gains moderate at its scale; the 0.4 figure is Switch's, not confirmed here. Keep the two ledgers straight.

What the experts actually learn

§7 traces a batch of tokens through ST-MoE-L, six sparse layers on each of the encoder and decoder, 32 experts each.

  • Encoder experts specialise. At every layer at least one expert takes sentinel tokens. Others group around punctuation, conjunctions and articles, verbs, visual and spatial descriptions, proper names, and counting or numbers.
  • Decoder experts do not. The entropy of sentinel-token routing is 3.4 at every decoder layer against a uniform value of 3.5 for 32 experts — effectively random. Encoder layers run 1.2 to 2.2.
  • Their explanation is structural, not semantic: fewer tokens are routed jointly in the decoder (group size 456 against 2048 in the encoder), and a higher proportion of decoder tokens are sentinels, so each decoder group covers a smaller semantic space. They float removing decoder experts entirely, which would also speed autoregressive decoding.
  • Multilingual experts specialise, but not by language. A group holds only 2–8 sequences and each sequence is one language, so load balancing forces every expert to handle every language even with 100+ languages in the corpus. A global load balance loss made both balance and quality worse.
⚠️ The specialisation tables are curated

The paper says so directly: the examples were extracted from many that showed no clear semantic or syntactic pattern. Treat encoder specialisation as real but partial evidence, and note that it is the routing entropy, not the token tables, that carries the encoder/decoder contrast quantitatively.

Switch says route to one expert, ST-MoE says two. Which is right?

Both, at their own scale — and the paper frames it as a revision rather than a disagreement. Switch's top-1 recommendation came from 220M-FLOP-matched models on 50B tokens. ST-MoE re-ran it at 1B-FLOP-matched on 100B tokens, roughly 8× the training compute, and found a small gain for routing to more than one expert, because the router's share of total computation shrinks as the model grows.

Then size it, because the honest answer is that it barely matters: +0.004 neg. log perp for top-2 over top-1 at capacity factor 1.25, where tripling a dense model is +0.090. Their own conclusion is that raising the capacity factor buys more quality than smarter routing does, and §9 uses this exact reversal as a warning about small-scale results.

Why not just clip the router logits instead of adding a loss term?

Because clipping runs after the roundoff error has already been committed. The logits are corrupted going into the exponential; clipping the corrupted value adds a discontinuity on top of the error rather than removing it. In their framing, clipping is itself a roundoff error. The z-loss makes the model produce small logits, which are accurately representable in the first place. Prevention, not correction.

Then flag the trap before it is set for you: the paper never runs the logit-clipping experiment. The clipping it does measure is Adafactor update clipping at clip = 0.1, which stabilises 3/3 runs but collapses quality to −4.206 against a −1.755 baseline. Different mechanism, and the only one with a number.

You have to fine-tune a sparse model on 250 examples. What do you do?

Expect to lose to the dense baseline, and say so — that is exactly the CB result. The sparse model reaches 100% train accuracy faster and still trails dense on held-out data at that size, while winning by several points on ReCoRD's 138k examples.

Levers, in the order the paper supports them: tune batch size and learning rate separately from the dense recipe, biasing to smaller batches and higher LR, because reusing dense hyperparameters can mask the entire pre-training gain. Set global dropout to 0.1 and raise expert dropout above it. Update the non-MoE parameters only, which costs nothing in quality and saves time and memory — but do not claim it generalises better, because the paper checked and it does not. Do not reach for high capacity factors: dropping 10–15% of tokens is roughly free at fine-tuning time.

Supervised fine-tuning narrows the gap without closing it.

The 145B is explicitly preliminary — 245B tokens on a warmup-and-constant schedule whose learning rate never decays, described as an initial study with the full run still to come. Do not quote it as a finished result.

The granularity ceiling is a systems constraint, not a quality one. Performance improves monotonically across everything tested (16 → 32 → 64 experts). 16B stops at expert size 0.25 because excessively small experts hurt computational efficiency, and the paper says finer granularity remains available above 16B. There is no measured optimum in this paper.

Why not split into a million tiny experts?

Not for the reason people usually give. Within everything DeepSeekMoE measured, finer is strictly better: 16, then 32, then 64 experts at fixed total and activated parameters improves performance at every step, and they report no turning point. The 145B goes further still, to expert size 0.125.

The binding constraint they name is computational efficiency — below a certain expert size the matrix multiplications stop using the hardware well, so 16B stops at 0.25× a standard FFN and the paper notes finer granularity is still available at larger scale. If you want to argue that quality itself peaks and turns over, you need a source outside this paper; DeepSeekMoE does not show it.

How much of the gain is segmentation and how much is the shared expert?

The paper does not separate them, and saying so is the better answer. Its ablation is a cumulative ladder — GShard, then plus a shared expert, then plus segmentation, then plus finer segmentation — with total and activated parameters held constant throughout. So sharing is only measured at coarse granularity and segmentation is only measured with sharing already in place.

What can be said with a number is that the shared expert is not optional at the final configuration: removing it while activating one more routed expert at equal compute moves Pile loss from 1.808 to 2.414. And that shared-to-routed ratio barely matters once you have one — 1, 2 and 4 shared experts give 1.808, 1.806 and 1.811.

Low-rank joint KV compression

Keys and values are compressed jointly into one latent vector, and that latent is the only thing cached (§2.1.2):

cKVt = WDKVht  →  kCt = WUKcKVt ,   vCt = WUVcKVt

V2 sets the KV compression dimension dc = 512. The two up-projections never execute during decode: they fold into the matrices on either side, so keys and values are never reconstructed at all. That fold is the whole trick, and everything in the next subsection exists to protect it.

⚠️ Body and appendix disagree on which matrix absorbs

§2.1.2 says WUK is absorbed into WQ. Appendix C says it is absorbed into WUQ. Quote Appendix C. Queries in V2 are themselves low-rank, so there is no bare WQ to absorb anything — the query path is WDQ down, then WUQ up. Both passages agree that WUV absorbs into WO.

⚠️ Query compression saves nothing at decode

V2 compresses queries too — cQt = WDQht with d′c = 1536 — and §2.1.2 says plainly that this is done even though it cannot reduce the KV cache. Queries are not cached; nothing about the cache changes. The payoff is activation memory during training, a different budget on a different axis. "MLA compresses Q, K and V to shrink the cache" is wrong on the Q.

Decoupled RoPE — the step that keeps the fold legal

RoPE and the absorption are incompatible, and this is the derivation worth having ready (§2.1.3). RoPE is position-sensitive on both queries and keys. Apply it to kCt and a position-dependent matrix — one that depends on the token being generated now — lands between the query projection and WUK. Matrix multiplication does not commute, so the fold becomes illegal and you must recompute keys for every prefix token at every step. That is precisely the cost MLA was built to remove.

The fix is to give position its own narrow channel: per-head decoupled queries qRt,i and a single decoupled key kRt shared across all 128 heads, each of per-head dimension dhR = 64, both carrying RoPE and both concatenated onto the compressed halves:

qt,i = [qCt,i ; qRt,i] ,   kt,i = [kCt,i ; kRt] ,   scale = √(dh + dhR)

Note the missing index on kRt: one per token, not one per head. It has to be cached alongside the latent, which is where the + dhR comes from — V2's total is (dc + dhR)l elements per token.

The efficiency numbers, and what is baked into them

Training: 172.8K GPU-hours per trillion tokens for V2 against 300.6K for DeepSeek 67B on the same H800 cluster — the 42.5% saving (§3.2.3). Inference: over 50K tokens/second generation throughput on a single node of 8 H800s, 5.76× DeepSeek 67B's maximum, with prompt input above 100K tokens/second.

The caveat travels with the number. 5.76× is a deployment measurement, not an architectural one: parameters are converted to FP8 and the KV cache is quantised to about 6 bits per element before it is taken, and throughput is measured on the prompt and generation length distribution of the already-deployed 67B service. Attributing all of it to MLA overstates the case.

Training infrastructure, for the same reason: HAI-LLM, 16-way zero-bubble pipeline parallelism, 8-way expert parallelism, ZeRO-1 data parallelism, and — because so few parameters are active — no tensor parallelism at all, which removes a large slice of communication (§3.1.3).

The connection to your other notes

FlashAttention attacks HBM traffic during the attention computation. GQA and MLA attack KV cache size during decode. Different bottlenecks, different phases — and V2 does not merely leave them compatible in principle, it composes them in practice: §3.1.3 states that MLA is optimised on top of an improved version of FlashAttention-2. Cite that line rather than hand-waving at compositionality.

The one thing to remember: MLA caches (dc + dhR)l instead of 2nhdhl — a cache that does not grow with head count — and decoupled RoPE exists solely so the up-projections can stay absorbed.

Limitations the authors state themselves
  • §5. No knowledge updates after pre-training; non-factual output and hallucination remain; the corpus is mainly Chinese and English, so proficiency in other languages may be limited and the paper says the model should be used with caution there.
  • §3.2.2. V2 saw fewer than a quarter of LLaMA3 70B's English tokens, and the authors acknowledge a slight remaining gap in basic English capability — MMLU 78.5 against 78.9.
  • §4.4. An alignment tax: RL lifts open-ended benchmarks but costs performance on standard ones such as BBH. Separately, dropping below 10K SFT instances produced a significant IFEval decline, against prior claims that fewer than 10K suffice.

None of these are MLA-specific — they are the paper's own caveats on the model MLA ships inside.

Why can't you just apply RoPE to MLA's compressed keys?

Because it destroys the only reason the compression is cheap at decode. MLA's saving comes from caching cKVt alone and folding WUK into WUQ so keys are never rebuilt. RoPE inserts a matrix that depends on the currently generating token between those two, and matrix multiplication does not commute, so the fold is no longer available. You would have to reconstruct keys for every prefix token at every decode step — the exact cost MLA exists to remove.

V2's answer is to route position through a separate channel: per-head decoupled queries plus one decoupled key shared by all 128 heads, dhR = 64 each, concatenated onto the compressed halves, with the softmax scale widened to √(dh + dhR). The decoupled key is cached as well, which is the + dhR term: 64 of V2's 576 elements per layer, roughly 11% of the cache, spent entirely on carrying position.

How much KV cache does MLA actually save, and against what?

Name the baseline first or the answer means nothing. Controlled, identically-trained pairs (Appendix D.2): MLA holds 14% of MHA's cache at ~16B and 4% at ~250B. V2's own configuration, derived from §3.1.2 rather than stated in the paper: 34,560 against 1,966,080 elements per token, or 1.76%. Against GQA the paper offers exactly one figure — V2's cache equals GQA with 2.25 groups.

The 93.3% in the abstract is a different comparison: DeepSeek-V2 against DeepSeek 67B, in KB per token, as deployed, with parameters in FP8 and the cache quantised to about 6 bits per element. It is a product claim rather than the architectural ratio, and it cannot be re-derived from this paper because 67B's attention dimensions are never given.

The reason the numbers spread is structural: MLA's cache is independent of head count, so the wider the model you compare against, the better the ratio looks.

Aux-loss-free load balancing — and the loss it still has

Every routed expert carries a bias bi. It is added to the affinity score for the purpose of ranking, and for nothing else (§2.1.2):

g′i,t = si,t  if  si,t + bi ∈ Topk({sj,t + bj}, Kr) ,  else 0

Read the right-hand side twice. The bias decides which experts fire; the gating value that multiplies the FFN output is the unmodified si,t. Expert load is monitored over the whole batch at every step, and at the end of the step each bias moves by γ — down if that expert was overloaded, up if it was underloaded. No gradient passes through the bias at all, which is the entire point: the balancing pressure never appears in the objective.

⚠️ "Auxiliary-loss-free" does not mean there is no auxiliary loss

V3 retains a complementary sequence-wise balance loss (§2.1.2, equations 17–20) to prevent extreme imbalance inside any single sequence, with balance factor α = 0.0001 — a value the paper describes as extremely small and justifies purely as insurance. The claim is that the auxiliary loss is no longer doing the balancing work, not that it has been removed. Saying V3 deleted the auxiliary loss, or that no gradient term competes with the LM loss at all, overstates the result the paper reports.

⚠️ Attribution — the strategy is Wang et al., not V3

§1 and §2.1.2 both cite Wang et al. (2024a), arXiv 2408.15664, for the auxiliary-loss-free strategy. V3's stated claim is that it pioneers the strategy at this scale, not that it originated it. Same error class as crediting DeepSeekMoE with the shared expert, or Switch with the router z-loss.

FP8 — what it costs, and what stays out of it

The headline is the error, and it is small: relative loss error consistently below 0.25% against a BF16 baseline, which the paper places well inside training randomness. The one thing that actually broke

Appendix B.2. Quantising activation gradients block-wise at 128×128, the way weights are quantised, diverges — on a roughly 16B MoE at around 300B tokens. Dgrad back-propagates in a chain to shallow layers and is highly sensitive to precision; the paper's hypothesis is token-correlated outliers that a block-wise scale cannot absorb. This is why activations are tiled 1×128 and transposed to 128×1 for the backward pass rather than simply blocked. It is the only divergence reported anywhere in the paper, and it is a useful counterweight to the claim that the training was uneventful.

Two consequences worth having ready. First, because the balancing holds, V3 drops no tokens — not in training, and not in inference, where redundant expert deployment keeps the load even. Second, because dispatch is capped by nodes rather than by experts, the interconnect is not charging for sparsity: V3 selects 8 routed experts and could select 13 at identical communication cost.

⚠️ V3 does not hold all experts in HBM — do not say that it does

The familiar follow-up is that you hold all the experts in memory but use a few, so MoE loves large batches and struggles at batch size 1. That is not what V3 deploys. Experts are sharded by expert parallelism throughout: 64-way EP across 8 nodes in training, EP32 at prefill with each GPU holding 8 experts plus 1 redundant, and EP320 at decode where each GPU hosts exactly one expert, with 64 GPUs carrying the redundant and shared experts.

The paper's statement about small batches is close to the opposite of struggle. At decode the batch per expert is usually within 256 tokens and the bottleneck is memory access rather than computation; since only one expert's parameters need loading, the memory overhead is minimal and only a small share of SMs need be given to dispatch and combine. If you want the memory argument, make it about the size of the deployment unit — 320 GPUs to decode — which is the limitation the authors state themselves.

The one thing to remember

V3's modelling change is one bias term per expert and one extra predicted token. Everything else that makes 671B trainable for 2.788M GPU-hours is systems work. MLA and DeepSeekMoE were both settled in V2, so sparsity is not the answer to "why is V3 interesting". The answer is that fine-grained MoE's communication cost — a computation-to-communication ratio of roughly 1:1 — is claimed to be fully hidden behind computation by DualPipe, a 4-node routing cap and an all-to-all implementation that fits in 20 of 132 SMs.

Is DeepSeek-V3 actually auxiliary-loss-free?

No, and the precise answer is better than either yes or no. The primary mechanism is loss-free: a per-expert bias added to affinity scores for top-K ranking only, moved by γ = 0.001 each step against measured batch load, with no gradient path through it. The gating value that multiplies the FFN output never sees the bias.

But V3 retains a complementary sequence-wise balance loss at α = 0.0001, to stop a single sequence collapsing. The paper sets it deliberately tiny and says so. The claim is that the auxiliary loss no longer does the balancing work, not that it is gone.

The sharper follow-up is what the win is attributable to. §4.5.3 builds a batch-wise auxiliary loss and it ties: 1B validation losses of 2.253 for both against 2.258 sequence-wise, and 2.080 for both against 2.085 at 3B. So the gain comes from balancing across the batch rather than within each sequence — which lets experts specialise by domain — and not from deleting the loss term at all.

Why doesn't MoE deliver its theoretical FLOP speedup?

Because every MoE layer pays for two all-to-alls — one to route tokens to the devices holding their experts, one to bring the results back — and on V3's cluster that is roughly half the time budget. §3.2.1 puts the computation-to-communication ratio for cross-node expert parallelism at about 1:1.

The numbers to quote are the bandwidths and the cap. NVLink runs at 160 GB/s against InfiniBand's 50 GB/s, a ratio of about 3.2, so V3 caps each token at 4 nodes to keep traffic off IB and lets it fan out over NVLink to an average of 3.2 experts per node. Under that cap the cost is set by node count rather than expert count: V3 selects 8 routed experts and could select 13 for the same price. The implementation is hand-written into 20 of the H800's 132 SMs as 10 channels, using warp specialisation and custom PTX.

What V3 claims is not that the cost is small but that it is hidden: DualPipe overlaps dispatch and combine with attention and MLP so both all-to-all and pipeline communication sit inside computation — at a price of two parameter copies and one extra unit of activation memory.

Do not finish with "and you hold all the experts in HBM". V3 shards them: 64-way expert parallelism in training, and one expert per GPU at decode across 320 GPUs, where the paper says the bottleneck is memory access rather than computation and the batch per expert is usually within 256 tokens.

How many tokens does MTP predict, and through what?

D = 1. Each position predicts the next token as usual plus one additional token, which §5.4.3 phrases as predicting the next 2 tokens. Same configuration, both phrasings in the same paper.

The mechanism is the part that gets probed. Not independent output heads — that is Gloeckle et al., which §2.2 names as the design V3 departs from. V3 uses sequential modules, each with its own Transformer block and projection Mk, each sharing the main model's one embedding layer and one output head. Depth k takes the depth k−1 representation, RMSNorms it, concatenates the RMSNormed embedding of token i+k, and projects. That preserves the full causal chain at every depth, which the parallel-head version cannot.

At inference the modules are discarded and the main model runs alone, which is why the ablation compares models of identical inference cost. Kept instead, they speculate: 85–90% acceptance on the second token, 1.8× TPS.

Hybrid attention — compress, then sparsify

§2.3. Attention becomes the dominant computational cost once context reaches extreme scales, so V4 attacks it twice over and interleaves two mechanisms layer by layer. CSA compresses the KV cache of every m tokens into one entry and then applies DeepSeek Sparse Attention over the compressed entries, so each query attends to only k of them. HCA compresses far harder, at m′ much greater than m, and then attends densely to what is left. The details that keep it working, and the one people miss

RoPE is partial, and applied twice. §2.3.3: only the last 64 dimensions of each query and each KV entry carry RoPE. Because a compressed entry is both key and value, the core attention output would otherwise carry absolute position, inherited from the weighted sum of KV entries. V4's countermeasure is to apply RoPE at position −i to the last 64 dimensions of each output head, restoring a relative encoding. That second application is the step that gets missed.

Attention sink. A learnable logit z′h per head enters the softmax denominator as Exp(z′h), so a head's attention scores need not sum to 1 and may sit near 0. Grouped output projection exists because c·nh is large: the nh outputs are split into g groups, each projected to dg, and the concatenation projected once more to d. RMSNorm is applied to every query head and to the single head of the compressed KV entries just before core attention, which matters again in the next subsection.

The V3 section above reports the unfused cost honestly: cross-node expert parallelism at a computation-to-communication ratio of roughly 1:1, InfiniBand at 50 GB/s against NVLink at 160 GB/s, a 4-node routing cap, and the collective hand-written into 20 of 132 SMs. V4 §3.1 profiles the same layer after fusing communication and computation into a single pipelined kernel, and reaches the opposite conclusion: within one MoE layer the total communication time is less than the computation time, so computation remains the bottleneck and the system tolerates lower interconnect bandwidth without degrading end-to-end performance.

Both are correct and the tension is the answer. "All-to-all is the MoE bottleneck" is a statement about an unfused schedule; V3 measures the cost, V4 measures what survives hiding it. The numbers: 1.50–1.73× measured speedup over strong non-fused baselines on general inference workloads and up to 1.96× on latency-sensitive ones such as RL rollout, against a 1.92× theoretical ceiling from Figure 5 evaluated on the Flash configuration, where Comet's is 1.42×. Validated on NVIDIA GPUs and HUAWEI Ascend NPUs; the kernel is open-sourced as MegaMoE inside DeepGEMM. Do not quote 1.92× as measured.

The balance point is worth being able to derive live. Each token-expert pair costs 6hd FLOPs — SwiGLU gate, up and down projections — against 3h bytes moved, FP8 on dispatch and BF16 on combine. The hidden size cancels:

C / B ≤ 6hd / 3h = 2d = 2 × 3072 = 6144 FLOPs per byte  →  1 GB/s of interconnect hides 6.1 TFLOP/s

Note which d that is: V4-Pro's expert intermediate dimension of 3072, not its hidden size of 7168. Past that ratio the interconnect stops being the constraint and additional bandwidth is dead silicon — which is what DeepSeek asks hardware vendors to target, rather than asking for more bandwidth.

mHC — a residual stream four wide, held non-expansive

§2.2. Hyper-Connections widen the residual stream from Rd to Rnhc×d and let three learned maps mix it: an input map Al, a residual transform Bl, an output map Cl.

Xl+1 = BlXl + ClFl(AlXl)

The layer Fl still receives a d-dimensional input, so widening the residual never touches the block's own shapes — which is what makes it a cheap extra scaling axis, since nhc is far smaller than d. The stated problem with plain HC is that stacking many layers frequently goes numerically unstable, which is what blocks scaling it. mHC's fix is a single constraint: Bl is projected onto the Birkhoff polytope, the manifold of doubly stochastic matrices, by Sinkhorn–Knopp iteration — exponentiate, then alternate row and column normalisation, tmax = 20. A doubly stochastic matrix has spectral norm at most 1, so the residual transform is non-expansive in both the forward pass and backpropagation, and the set is closed under multiplication, so deep stacks stay bounded as well. Al and Cl are bounded separately through a sigmoid on their raw parameters, Al = σ(·) and Cl = 2σ(·), non-negative so the branches cannot cancel each other out. All three are generated dynamically, as an input-dependent component plus a learnable static bias, gated by a learnable factor initialised small.

The cost is stated: 6.7% of the overlapped 1F1B pipeline stage in wall time (§3.4.2), paid down with fused kernels, selective recomputation of inter-layer hidden states and normalised layer inputs, and an adjusted DualPipe schedule. DualPipe survives from V3 and is modified, not replaced. With nhc = 4 in both models, the three maps together produce 4 + 16 + 4 outputs, which is why §3.3 has to design around an mHC matrix multiplication whose output dimension is only 24.

Muon, and the two things that stopped the loss spikes

Muon replaces AdamW for most modules, for stated reasons of faster convergence and improved training stability. Orthogonalisation uses a hybrid Newton–Schulz of 10 iterations in two stages: 8 steps at (a, b, c) = (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315) to drive the singular values close to 1, then 2 steps at (2, −1.5, 0.5) to settle them there precisely. Momentum 0.95, weight decay 0.1, and the RMS of each update matrix rescaled to 0.18 so the AdamW learning rate transfers. No QK-Clip, and the reason is architectural rather than a preference: V4 applies RMSNorm directly to the attention queries and KV entries, which already prevents attention logits from exploding, so the mechanism the Kimi tab needs is unnecessary here.

The instability, and two fixes the authors say they cannot explain

§4.2.3. Loss spikes were consistently tied to outliers in the MoE layers, with the routing mechanism appearing to amplify them. Rollbacks restored the run but did not prevent recurrence, so two techniques were added.

Anticipatory Routing decouples the routing network from the backbone in time: features are computed with the current parameters θt while routing indices come from θt−Δt, pre-computed and cached one step ahead so the parameters need not be loaded twice. Overhead is bounded to roughly 20% of wall-clock time, which is why it is triggered automatically only after a spike is detected and a short rollback, then switched off again — that dynamic application, not the technique itself, is what keeps the net cost negligible. SwiGLU clamping holds the linear component to [−10, 10] and caps the gate component at 10.

The framing is the quotable part. The paper says plainly that a comprehensive theoretical understanding of why either works remains open, and §6 repeats it as a limitation. Explaining the mechanism confidently is the error here.

The one thing to remember

V4 buys its million-token context by compressing the KV cache before it sparsifies it, and the two reductions multiply. CSA folds every m = 4 tokens into one entry and then keeps only the top k; HCA folds every m′ = 128 into one and attends to all of them; the layers alternate. That is where 27% of V3.2's single-token FLOPs and 10% of its KV cache come from — not from the MoE, which is the part that did not change. DeepSeekMoE, auxiliary-loss-free balancing, MTP at depth 1 and DualPipe all survive from V3 intact.

What actually changed between V3 and V4, and why?

Answer with two lists, because the unchanged one is shorter and more surprising. Carried over unchanged in kind: DeepSeekMoE with fine-grained routed plus shared experts; auxiliary-loss-free balancing, still with the complementary sequence-wise balance loss at α = 0.0001; MTP, which §2 says is configured identically to V3's and which §4.2.1 sets at depth 1; and DualPipe, which §3.4.2 adjusts to absorb mHC's extra pipeline traffic rather than replacing.

Changed, with the paper's stated reason attached: attention becomes hybrid CSA plus HCA, because attention is the dominant computational bottleneck once context reaches extreme scales; the residual becomes mHC, because plain Hyper-Connections go numerically unstable when stacked and the doubly stochastic constraint makes the residual map non-expansive; the optimizer becomes Muon, for faster convergence and improved stability.

Then name the changes that come with no reason, because volunteering that is worth more than inventing one. The MoE affinity activation moves from sigmoid to Sqrt(Softplus(·)); the cap on the number of routing target nodes is removed and the parallelism strategy redesigned around it; and the initial dense FFN blocks become MoE layers using hash routing on the input token ID. The report states each of these plainly and explains none of them.

Where does DeepSeek-V4's 27% of V3.2's inference FLOPs come from?

Compression and sparsity multiplied together, then precision on top. CSA cuts the sequence length to 1/m with m = 4 before selecting anything, so at 1M tokens a CSA layer sees 250,000 compressed entries and then attends to the top 1,024 of those in V4-Pro. HCA cuts to 1/m′ with m′ = 128, leaving roughly 7,800 entries, and attends to all of them densely. The two alternate, so the model pays neither cost in every layer.

Three smaller contributions, all §2.3.4: KV entries are stored in BF16 only on the RoPE dimensions and FP8 elsewhere, which nearly halves the cache before compression is counted; the lightning indexer computes its attention in FP4; and V4's attention top-k is deliberately smaller than V3.2's, which helps short and medium sequences rather than long ones.

Two things to keep straight while saying it. The 27% is V4-Pro's FLOPs figure — Flash's is 10%, and 10% is also Pro's cache figure. And every one of these ratios is measured in equivalent FP8 FLOPs against DeepSeek-V3.2 at 1M context: a different baseline from the V3 in this tab, and a ratio that shrinks as the context gets shorter.

R1-Zero — what is verified, and with which numbers

Pure RL, no SFT cold start: confirmed. §2 states R1-Zero relies exclusively on RL without supervised fine-tuning, and §1 states the SFT phase before RL is deliberately bypassed, on the hypothesis that human-defined reasoning patterns cap exploration. The base checkpoint is DeepSeek-V3-Base.

GRPO: confirmed as the algorithm, but it is not R1's. GRPO is Shao et al. 2024, introduced in DeepSeekMath; R1 adopts it for both R1-Zero and R1. It drops PPO's value model and normalises the advantage within a sampled group of G outputs per question, and adds an unbiased KL estimator directly to the loss rather than as a per-token dense reward.

Ai = (ri − mean{r1…rG}) / std{r1…rG}      Rewardrule = Rewardacc + Rewardformat

The accuracy and format rewards carry equal weight. The paper abstains from neural reward models — outcome-based or process-based — on reasoning tasks, because they get reward-hacked under large-scale RL and retraining them complicates the pipeline. That refusal, not the choice of optimiser, is what makes the loop work.

MuonClip

K2 was pre-trained on 15.5T tokens with the Muon optimiser and zero loss spike. Getting there required a fix.

⚠️ "Zero loss spike" is not "zero instability"

The paper's claim is about the loss curve: 15.5T tokens with no observable spikes, shown in Figure 3 without smoothing or sub-sampling. It is not a claim that nothing went wrong. Appendix D reports that QK-Clip fired on 12.7% of attention heads at least once during the first 70,000 steps, clamping Smax to 100; past that point every head had brought its own max logit below the threshold and the mechanism went inert. Instability was suppressed, not absent — and the suppressed version is the more interesting answer.

The evidence that there was something to suppress: a mid-scale probe at 9B activated / 53B total under vanilla Muon drove maximum attention logits past magnitude 1000 — a level that usually produces loss spikes and occasional divergence. K2 itself then ran MuonClip at τ = 100: logits were capped at 100 early and decayed to a normal operating range after roughly 30% of training steps, with no adjustment to τ.

What QK-Clip does: after the optimiser step, if a head's max logit exceeds τ, rescale that head's query and key projections by γh = min(1, τ/Shmax). Per head, not per layer, because only a small subset of heads explode. For MLA the clip touches only the unshared components:

qC, kC (head-specific) × √γh  ·  qR (head-specific rotary) × γh  ·  kR (shared rotary) untouched

The shared rotary key is left alone precisely because scaling it would reach across every head. QK-Norm, the obvious alternative, is unavailable here for a structural reason worth naming: MLA does not fully materialise its key matrices at inference. Logit soft-capping is rejected separately, because the query–key dot products can grow arbitrarily before the cap applies — the cap hides the symptom and leaves the weights growing.

Pairs with ST-MoE

Two eras of the same problem: ST-MoE's z-loss controls router logits; MuonClip controls attention logits under Muon. Both are "keep the thing that goes into an exponential small." Good pairing to mention if asked about training stability — and it connects to your optimizers note.

K3 stability and the optimiser

Muon again, but note the wording. K3 says it retains "the weight-clipping mechanism introduced in Kimi K2" and never names QK-Clip or MuonClip. Quote it that way; the specific mechanism is K2's to describe.

The new piece is per-head Muon. Instead of running Newton–Schulz orthogonalisation over the full Q, K and V projection matrices, K3 partitions their momentum matrices along the head dimension and orthogonalises each head's block separately. Stated reason: full-matrix orthogonalisation treats all heads as a single coupled block, so heads with larger gradient or momentum scales dominate the shared update direction while smaller-scale heads receive insufficiently normalised updates. Claimed effects: more balanced learning dynamics across heads, better training stability at larger scales, and slightly lower optimiser overhead, since Newton–Schulz on tall per-head blocks is cheaper than on the full projection matrix.

Two failure modes attributed directly to extreme sparsity at 2.8T scale, each with its fix:

  • Exploding activations in the routed branch. The routed path composes W↓, a gated multi-branch expert FFN, and W↑ into nearly four consecutive matrix multiplications; that ill-conditioned chain at 2.78T parameters produces exploding internal activations. Fix: an RMSNorm inserted between expert aggregation and the up-projection, plus SiTU-GLU. K3 reports the extra RMSNorm also improves validation loss and downstream benchmarks, not just stability.
  • Load balancing past the aux-loss-free regime. The fixed-step sign update on the expert bias stops behaving well at nearly 103 experts per layer. Fix: Quantile Balancing sets each expert's bias from the router-score quantile matching its target load q = mk/n, derived in a single forward pass, with no learning-rate-like hyperparameter. At scale the quantile is read from a per-expert histogram with B = 1000 bins, bounding the error at a few 10−3 for under 1% of the communication cost of exchanging raw margins.

Three more stability decisions, all numeric: attention output is kept in FP32 during training to correct the biased rounding error in flash attention; the KDA log-decay is lower-bounded at gmin = −5, so every retention factor exceeds e−5 ≈ 6.7×10−3 and the reciprocal rescaling factor stays under e80, inside BF16 dynamic range; and MXFP4 quantisation-aware training runs on the MoE expert weights with MXFP8 activations through both SFT and RL, non-expert components staying in higher precision. Learning rate is cosine with a 1% linear warm-up, weight decay 0.1 — K3's scaling-law study searched hyperparameters independently for each schedule and found cosine reaches a lower final loss than WSD at its own optimum, reversing K2's choice.

Questions to answer out loud

Is a fixed FLOP budget the same as a fixed training time?

No. FLOPs is a count of operations; FLOP/s is a rate. FLOPs = FLOP/s × seconds. Scaling laws use FLOPs because it is hardware-agnostic and survives the next chip generation.

But FLOPs is a lossy proxy for cost: it captures matmul arithmetic and misses memory bandwidth, collectives, data loading, and kernel efficiency. Your own FlashAttention backward is the cleanest counterexample — it increases FLOPs by recomputing S and P, yet decreases wall-clock, because attention is memory-bound. A pure FLOP budget scores that as a loss.

Sardana et al. put a number on the same gap in the other direction: at 2T inference tokens a Chinchilla-70B is 1.3% off optimal in FLOPs but 36% off in dollars, because generation runs at ~1% MFU against ~50% for training.

Why did Kaplan and Chinchilla disagree?

Kaplan held the LR schedule fixed at a long horizon and read intermediate losses off those curves. You can derive the horizon from Kaplan §2.2 without citing Chinchilla: every run is a fixed 2.5e5 steps at 512 sequences of 1024 tokens, so 2.5e5 × 219 = 1.31e11 tokens — the 130B Chinchilla §2 names. Loss is minimised only when schedule length matches token count, so those intermediate points overestimate the achievable loss at smaller D, understating the value of training on fewer tokens and biasing toward growing N faster than D. Chinchilla's Appendix B quantifies the sensitivity: overshoot the intended step count by more than 25% and final loss visibly degrades.

Kaplan checked the schedule and cleared it — §D.6 finds the choice mostly irrelevant — but only for runs taken to convergence, which is the one regime where the artefact does not appear.

Secondary: many of Kaplan's runs were under 100M params, and there is curvature in the frontier that only appears larger.

Then close it with the experiment. Chinchilla §D.4 trained both predictions head to head at 1e21 FLOPs — Kaplan's 4.68B (they trained 4.74B) against Approach 1's 2.86B (they trained 2.80B), matched depth-to-width. The smaller model won.

Which currency is the question in?

Three of them, and noticing which one applies is usually the actual test. Loss at fixed training FLOPs is Chinchilla. Total FLOPs at fixed loss is Sardana et al.'s compute-optimal analysis — savings of 2.6–16%. Dollars at fixed loss is their cost-optimal analysis — savings of 34–58%, because utilisation differs by 50× between a generated token and a trained one.

Adding a fourth: latency at fixed quality, where MoE stops helping entirely because batch size 1 cannot amortise expert loading.

Why does MoE need a load-balancing loss, and what is the cost?

Routing is self-reinforcing: an expert receiving more tokens improves faster and attracts more, collapsing the rest to dead weight. The aux loss pushes toward uniform utilisation. The cost is that it directly opposes the LM loss — an explicit regulariser degrading the objective you care about.

Switch's coefficient sweep is the evidence that the tension is real: α from 1e-1 to 1e-5, settling on 1e-2 as large enough to balance load and small enough not to overwhelm cross-entropy. DeepSeek-V3's learned router bias achieves balance without any gradient conflict.

Why is the z-loss better than clipping router logits?

Ordering. Clipping happens after roundoff has already occurred and adds a larger discontinuity on top — clipping is itself a roundoff error. The z-loss makes the model produce small logits in the first place, so they are accurately representable entering the exponential. Prevention, not correction.

And keep the lineage straight: the z-loss is ST-MoE. Switch's answer to the same instability was selective precision — float32 inside the router body only, recast before the all-to-all — because full bfloat16 diverges outright.

What is the shared expert for?

It absorbs the knowledge every token needs, so routed experts do not each spend capacity relearning the basics and can specialise properly. DeepSeekMoE's argument is about redundancy and parameter efficiency, not about insuring against bad routing.

There is a number for how load-bearing it is: disable the shared expert, activate one more routed expert to hold computation constant, and Pile loss moves from 1.808 to 2.414 — worse than a hash-routing baseline. Routed capacity does not substitute for it. The ratio matters far less than the presence: 1, 2 and 4 shared experts of 64 give 1.808, 1.806 and 1.811.

Credit carefully. §3.2 attributes the prototype to Rajbhandari et al. (2022) and claims only that DeepSeekMoE reaches it from an algorithmic rather than an engineering direction.

When would you not use MoE?

Batch-size-1 latency-critical serving (no batching to amortise expert loading); memory-constrained deployment (all experts must be resident); when fine-tuning on small downstream data is the main use case (ST-MoE's overfitting finding, which Switch's expert dropout only partly mitigates); and where all-to-all cost dominates, e.g. thin interconnects.

Given 10× compute, what do you build?

Chinchilla: ~3.2× params, ~3.2× tokens. Then ask the follow-up questions yourself — how much will this be served (pushes smaller and longer; the shift becomes material as inference volume approaches training tokens), is there enough data (4-epoch rule), does it fit the cluster and the deadline (C / throughput).

You are out of data. What is the order of operations?

Repeat before you widen. Muennighoff's fitted decay constants are R*D = 15.39 for repeated tokens against R*N = 5.31 for parameters beyond the compute-optimal count, so at equal FLOPs a repeated token holds its value about three times longer than an extra parameter. Four epochs costs about 0.5% of loss; sixteen is where returns collapse; and the hard ceiling is that no amount of repetition beats a single epoch on 16.4× your unique tokens.

Then buy tokens you do not have. Code to 50% of the budget is free on natural-language tasks and doubles effective tokens; perplexity filtering pays on noisy corpora and deduplication did not pay on C4. Stack the first two and you have 8× the tokens at roughly the quality of 8× the unique data.

Caveat worth volunteering: the law cannot represent excess parameters or epochs actively hurting, only plateauing, and empirically they do hurt.

What did FP8 cost DeepSeek-V3 in accuracy, and how do we know?

Relative loss error consistently below 0.25% against a BF16 baseline. The controls are sub-scale: Appendix B.1 gives approximately 16B total parameters on 1.33T tokens, and approximately 230B on around 0.9T. §3.3 describes the same validation as roughly 1 trillion tokens at scales similar to DeepSeek-V2-Lite and DeepSeek-V2 — quote the appendix. There is no BF16 control at 671B and the paper does not claim one.

The mitigations that buy the 0.25%: 1×128 tiles for activations and 128×128 blocks for weights, E4M3 on every tensor rather than the usual E4M3/E5M2 split, and promotion of partial sums to FP32 in CUDA cores every 128 elements — needed because H800 Tensor Cores retain only about 14 bits, worth nearly 2% maximum relative error at K = 4096. Appendix B.2 reports the one failure: block-wise quantisation of activation gradients diverges on a roughly 16B model at around 300B tokens.

What does DualPipe cost, and what does it buy?

It buys a bubble of (PP/2 − 1)(F&B + B − 3W) against 1F1B's (PP − 1)(F + B) and ZB1P's (PP − 1)(F + B − 2W), and it hides both all-to-all and pipeline communication inside computation — the point, given that cross-node expert parallelism runs at roughly a 1:1 computation-to-communication ratio.

It costs two copies of the model parameters and one extra unit of activation memory, PP + 1 against PP. V3's defence is that the duplication is affordable because the expert-parallel size is already large. Structurally it needs only pipeline stages and micro-batches divisible by 2, and neither bubbles nor activation memory grow as the micro-batch count rises.

R1-Zero already reasons. Why build R1 at all?

Readability and scope. R1-Zero mixes English and Chinese inside a single chain of thought, and its rule-based RL is narrow: reasoning tasks only, weak on writing and open-domain QA. IF-Eval 46.6 and AlpacaEval 2.0 24.7 are the numbers.

Then give the cost honestly. R1 reaches 83.3 and 87.6 on those two, and gives ground back on GPQA Diamond (75.8 to 71.5) and CNMO (88.1 to 78.8). §G.1 concludes RL and SFT are both indispensable. R1-Zero is the finding, R1 is the product.

Where does R1's reasoning actually come from?

Outcome-based RL against a verifier, on a base checkpoint already large enough to use long chains. The reward is rule-based only, accuracy plus format at equal weight, and the paper explicitly declines neural reward models on reasoning because they get reward-hacked at scale.

The two stated conditions are a reliable verifier and sufficient base capacity: §G.1 reports a 7B dense and a 16B MoE base failing outright under the same recipe, with 32B, 230B and 671B succeeding. GRPO is the optimiser, not the source of the behaviour.

Why GRPO instead of PPO?

Cost and fit. PPO carries a value model of roughly policy size, so memory and compute roughly double, and that value model has to predict returns from partial responses, which is badly posed for long CoT where later tokens revise earlier ones. GRPO estimates the advantage from group statistics and drops the value model entirely.

Do not oversell it. Supplementary A.3's own comparison on MATH, using DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite (16B MoE, 2.4B active), has PPO at λ = 1.0 nearly matching GRPO, and only the default λ = 0.95 trailing badly. The honest claim is cheaper and less tuning-sensitive, not more capable.

Sparsity went 32 to 48 to 56. Is the ceiling in sight?

Not in the ratio, and say so first: Switch-C is 1 of 2048, DeepSeek-V3 8 of 256 = 32, K2 8 of 384 = 48, K3 16 of 896 = 56, and K2 states outright that its scaling analysis told it to keep going.

Then puncture it, because the interesting part is the second derivative. K2 → K3 moved the ratio +17% while the expert pool grew 133% and activated experts 100%. The ratio is the slowest-moving number in that row, which is what you would expect if the cheap gains were behind us.

And the cost is now itemised. K3 attributes two failure modes to extreme sparsity — exploding activations in a routed path that composes nearly four consecutive matmuls, and load balancing beyond the regime where fixed-step sign updates on the expert bias behave — and spends three new components on them: RMSNorm before the up-projection, SiTU-GLU, Quantile Balancing. K2 needed none of that at 48. The defensible sentence is that the ratio has no known ceiling but the engineering per step up is visible for the first time.

ST-MoE already had the z-loss. Why did K2 need something new?

Different logits. The z-loss penalises the router logit magnitude; K2's problem is the attention logit, and it appears under Muon specifically — more often with Muon than AdamW in their experiments. Both are the same instinct, keep the input to an exponential small, applied at different places.

The two obvious off-the-shelf fixes both fail here, and naming why is the answer. Logit soft-capping clips after the fact, so the query–key dot products still grow arbitrarily before the cap binds — symptom, not cause. QK-Norm is structurally unavailable under MLA, because the key matrices are not fully materialised at inference.

So QK-Clip rescales the weights instead: γh = min(1, τ/Shmax) applied post-update, per head rather than per layer because only a small subset of heads explode, and for MLA only on the unshared components — qC, kC by √γh, qR by γh, shared kR left alone so one head's fix does not reach the others. K2 ran τ = 100, and the mechanism self-deactivated after roughly 70,000 steps.

How many tokens was Kimi K3 trained on?

Not published. That is the whole answer, and volunteering it is worth more than a guess.

What the report does give: the corpus by domain (Web Text, Code, Mathematics, Knowledge, plus a vision corpus), a statement that batch size, learning rate, tokens-per-parameter ratio and model shape were all retuned by a dedicated scaling-law study, and the context curriculum — 8K to 64K during pre-training, 256K to 1M during cooldown, four stages. No token count, and no TPP value to back one out of.

The trap is reconstructing one from K2's 15.5T and the 2.67× parameter ratio. K3 explicitly says the optimal training regime changed, so the K2 ratio is the wrong prior, and the answer would be invented.

Is all-to-all still the MoE bottleneck?

Conditionally, and naming the condition is the answer. Unfused, yes: DeepSeek-V3 §3.2.1 puts cross-node expert parallelism at roughly a 1:1 computation-to-communication ratio, with InfiniBand at 50 GB/s against NVLink at 160 GB/s, a 4-node cap per token, and the collective hand-written into 20 of 132 SMs. Fused, no: DeepSeek-V4 §3.1 profiles one MoE layer and finds total communication time below total computation time once dispatch, both GEMMs and combine are scheduled into a single pipelined kernel in expert waves — 1.50–1.73× over non-fused baselines, up to 1.96× on RL rollout.

Two papers two years apart, and no contradiction: V3 measures the cost, V4 measures what is left after hiding it. V3's own claim was already that DualPipe hides the cost rather than removing it; V4's fused kernel is the same instinct pushed one level down.

The useful consequence is the balance point. With 6hd FLOPs of compute against 3h bytes moved per token-expert pair, the hidden size cancels and the requirement is C/B ≤ 2d — 6144 FLOPs per byte at V4-Pro's expert intermediate dimension of 3072, so 1 GB/s of interconnect hides 6.1 TFLOP/s. Above that, more bandwidth buys nothing, which is what DeepSeek tells hardware vendors instead of asking for more of it.

Do not finish on HBM residency either. V3 shards experts — one per GPU across 320 at decode — and V4 moves routed expert weights to FP4 precisely because they dominate GPU memory.

MLA answered KV cache size. What answered it next?

Compression along the sequence dimension, which is the axis MLA never touched. MLA shrinks the per-token cache to (dc + dhR)l and makes it independent of head count, but it still stores one entry per token, so the cache grows linearly with context and each query still attends to everything. At a million tokens that is the binding constraint.

DeepSeek-V4's answer is two mechanisms interleaved layer by layer. CSA folds every m = 4 tokens into one KV entry and then applies sparse selection over the compressed entries, so a query attends to a fixed top-k rather than to the whole prefix. HCA folds every m′ = 128 into one and attends densely to what remains. Both use a shared-KV MQA core in which one compressed entry serves as key and value at once.

The ladder worth being able to recite: MHA → GQA/MQA cut head count and cost quality; MLA cut the per-token state without costing quality; DSA cut how many tokens are attended to; CSA and HCA cut how many entries exist at all, then apply sparsity on top of that. Each step attacks a different term, which is why they compose rather than compete.

The whole thing in eight sentences
  • Loss follows power laws in params, data and compute, and scale beats architecture.
  • Compute-optimal means scaling params and tokens equally — Kaplan's disagreement traces to one LR-schedule artefact, and was settled by a head-to-head at 1e21 FLOPs.
  • The iso-loss contours are flat near the optimum, so being modestly off is cheap — 1.3% of FLOPs for a 70B at 2T inference tokens.
  • That flatness is why inference-aware training deliberately overshoots on tokens, and why the real saving shows up in dollars (34–58%) rather than FLOPs (2.6–16%).
  • Data repetition is nearly free to about four epochs, then it is not.
  • MoE adds a fourth axis: more parameters at constant FLOPs per example.
  • Sparse models need explicit stability work — selective precision in Switch, z-loss for routers in ST-MoE — and the balance loss fights the LM loss until you replace it with a bias.
  • The real MoE bottleneck is HBM residency, and all-to-all communication unless you fuse it — V3 measures roughly 1:1 compute-to-comms unfused, V4 measures communication below computation inside a fused layer.
