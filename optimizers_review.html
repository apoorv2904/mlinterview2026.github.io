The one derivation

Take a first-order Taylor expansion around the current weights:

L(w + Δ) ≈ L(w) + ⟨g, Δ⟩

Minimising this over all Δ is meaningless — it is linear, so it runs off to −∞. You must add a leash. Pick a norm ‖·‖, pick a trust radius r, and solve:

Δ* = argmin‖Δ‖ ≤ r ⟨g, Δ⟩

The answer is always "go as far as the leash allows, in the direction most anti-aligned with g". The norm you choose is the optimizer. That is the entire taxonomy, and it is the one thing worth memorising.

If you can do this on a whiteboard you can rebuild all four optimizers from scratch — the next tab works each line out in full, including where the step size comes from, why the corners of the ball are what you always land on, and how the choice of norm decides which tensors Muon may touch.

The three knobs

1. Which norm (geometry). Covered above. ℓ₂ treats every scalar weight as interchangeable. ℓ∞ says "the risky thing is any one coordinate moving too far" — which is what you want when different layers have gradients differing by orders of magnitude. Spectral says "a weight matrix is a linear map; the risky thing is stretching some input direction too much" — the right object for hidden layers.

2. How much smoothing. g is a one-minibatch sample of the true gradient. An exponential moving average with coefficient β averages roughly 1/(1−β) recent samples, cutting variance at the cost of staleness. β = 0.9 → ~10 steps; β = 0.95 → ~20; β = 0.999 → ~1000. That single formula explains every β you will ever be asked about.

3. Where weight decay lives. Inside the loss (L2 penalty, so it flows through the preconditioner) or bolted onto the update (decoupled). These are identical when the preconditioner is the identity, and different otherwise — which is the entire content of the Adam → AdamW story.

Note — why square roots are everywhere in this material

Adam has √v̂. Muon has √max(A,B), √min(A,B), √(d_out/d_in). These are not unrelated fudge factors — every one is the same conversion: a sum of squares turned back into a typical magnitude.

Squares are what you get when you measure size the natural way (variance, energy, Euclidean length all accumulate as Σx²). But you need to divide by or compare against something in the original units, so you take a root to get back.

Adam accumulates g², so it must take a square root before dividing m by it — otherwise the update is in the wrong units and the scale-invariance argument collapses. The compression

direction (norm) × smoothing (momentum) × decay (coupled or not). Four optimizers, three knobs. Everything else in this document is a consequence, a default value, or a byte count.

2. The derivation, in full

Why you need a leash at all

You know the loss and its gradient at one point. The only honest local model you have is the linear one:

L(w + Δ) ≈ L(w) + ⟨g, Δ⟩

Minimising that over all Δ gives −∞: a linear function has no minimum. The model is only trustworthy near w anyway, so restrict Δ to a small ball and minimise there:

Δ* = argmin‖Δ‖ ≤ r ⟨g, Δ⟩

Two decisions are hiding in that line, and it pays to keep them apart:

  • Which norm ‖·‖ — i.e. what shape is the ball. This decides the direction, and it is what distinguishes SGD from Adam from Muon.
  • How big is r — this decides the length, and it is what the learning rate is really doing. Covered in § step size below.

The one theorem

Define the dual norm of g: the largest inner product g can achieve against a unit-ball vector.

‖g‖★ := max‖Δ‖ ≤ 1 ⟨g, Δ⟩

By construction, ⟨g,Δ⟩ ≥ −r‖g‖★ for every Δ in the ball of radius r. So the problem has the same shape every time:

   value at the optimum   :   ⟨g, Δ*⟩ = −r · ‖g‖★      ← predicted decrease in loss
   the optimum itself     :   Δ*      = −r · lmo(g)     ← lmo = the maximiser above

So the whole exercise is: name the dual norm, then write down the Δ that achieves equality. That maximiser has several names in the literature — the linear minimisation oracle (LMO), the dualiser, the sharp operator — all the same object.

ℓ∞ℓ₁cube
ℓpℓq, with 1/p + 1/q = 1—

Two dual pairs people flip

The dual of ℓ∞ is ℓ₁, not ℓ∞. And ‖·‖₂ on a matrix conventionally means the spectral norm (the operator 2-norm), not the Frobenius norm. Frobenius is the ℓ₂ norm of the flattened matrix, and steepest descent under it is just SGD — the entire content of Muon is that spectral ≠ Frobenius.

Case 1 — ℓ₂ gives SGD

   minimise   ⟨g, Δ⟩        subject to   ‖Δ‖₂ ≤ r

   Cauchy–Schwarz:   ⟨g, Δ⟩  ≥  −‖g‖₂ · ‖Δ‖₂  ≥  −r‖g‖₂

   equality needs:   (i)  Δ parallel to g, pointing the other way
                     (ii) ‖Δ‖₂ = r  (saturate the leash)

   ⇒   Δ* = −r · g/‖g‖₂           dual norm value: ‖g‖★ = ‖g‖₂

ℓ₂ is self-dual, which is why this case looks trivial and why it took decades for anyone to ask what happens with a different norm. Note the literal answer is normalised gradient descent — the direction of g, at fixed length r. Plain SGD's −ηg comes from the next section.

Where the step size comes from

The hard leash gives a direction and a length r that you still have to choose. The alternative is to replace the leash with a quadratic upper bound. If the loss is β-smooth in the same norm:

   L(w + Δ)  ≤  L(w) + ⟨g, Δ⟩ + (β/2)·‖Δ‖₂²

   minimise the right-hand side over Δ  ⇒  Δ = −g/β     ⇒     η = 1/β

The learning rate is one over the sharpness. And this is exactly a trust region with r = ‖g‖₂/β — a leash whose length is proportional to the gradient norm. That is the entire difference between normalised and unnormalised SGD, and it generalises: the norm gives you the direction, a curvature model gives you the length.

Note — why this matters for the other two cases

Run the same argument under ℓ∞ and you get a fixed-size step in every coordinate — signSGD with a constant learning rate, no normalisation needed. Under the spectral norm you get Muon's fixed-size orthogonal step. In all three, the "adaptive" feel comes from the geometry of the ball, not from any explicit per-parameter learning rate.

Case 2 — ℓ∞ gives sign descent (and Adam)

Now the ball is a cube: ‖Δ‖∞ ≤ r means |Δᵢ| ≤ r for each coordinate independently. The objective is a sum over coordinates, so the problem separates — solve each coordinate on its own:

   per coordinate:   minimise  gᵢ·Δᵢ   over   Δᵢ ∈ [−r, +r]

                     a linear function on an interval is minimised at an endpoint

   ⇒  Δᵢ* = −r·sign(gᵢ),  contributing  −r·|gᵢ|

   total:  ⟨g, Δ*⟩ = −r·Σ|gᵢ| = −r‖g‖₁      ← confirms the dual is ℓ₁

Read what happened: the solution sits at a corner of the cube, so every entry is ±r. The magnitude |gᵢ| influences nothing except the sign. A coordinate with gradient 10⁻⁶ takes the same size step as one with gradient 10³.

That is precisely the property Adam inherits, and precisely why it works on transformers, where embedding, attention, MLP, and LayerNorm gradients differ by orders of magnitude and no single global step size can serve all of them.

Note — Adam is the soft version, and that is deliberate

Exact ℓ∞ steepest descent is signSGD, which is brittle: at gᵢ = 0 the solution is undefined (any Δᵢ works), and a single noisy sign flip produces a full-size step in the wrong direction. Adam's m̂/(√v̂ + ε) is a continuous relaxation of sign: it saturates toward ±1 when the gradient is consistent across steps, and shrinks toward 0 when the sign is noisy (large √v, small |m|). Same geometry, with a confidence weighting bolted on.

Case 3 — the spectral norm gives Muon

Now the variable is a matrix G ∈ ℝm×n and the inner product is the Frobenius one, ⟨G,Δ⟩ = tr(GᵀΔ). The constraint ‖Δ‖spectral ≤ r says: every singular value of Δ is at most r.

   von Neumann's trace inequality:   |tr(AᵀB)|  ≤  Σᵢ σᵢ(A)·σᵢ(B)

   so    ⟨G, Δ⟩  ≥  −Σᵢ σᵢ(G)·σᵢ(Δ)  ≥  −r·Σᵢ σᵢ(G)  =  −r·‖G‖_nuclear

   equality needs:   (i)  every σᵢ(Δ) = r          (saturate, in every direction)
                     (ii) Δ shares G's singular vectors, oppositely aligned

   write  G = UΣVᵀ    ⇒    Δ* = −r · UVᵀ

Verify it in one line: ⟨G, −r·UVᵀ⟩ = −r·tr(VΣUᵀUVᵀ) = −r·tr(VΣVᵀ) = −r·Σσᵢ. ✓ And UVᵀ is semi-orthogonal, so its spectral norm is exactly 1. ✓

So the answer keeps U and V and replaces Σ with the identity. That object has several names — the polar factor of G (from the polar decomposition G = (UVᵀ)(VΣVᵀ)), the matrix sign msign(G), or the zeroth matrix power G(GᵀG)−1/2. Newton–Schulz is just a cheap way to compute it approximately with matmuls.

Note — the whole difference in one 2×2 
   G = ⎡10   0 ⎤        singular values 10 and 0.1
       ⎣ 0  0.1⎦

   SGD  step ∝ ⎡10  0 ⎤     the second direction gets a 100× smaller step
               ⎣ 0 0.1⎦     — it will take ~100× as many steps to learn

   Muon step ∝ ⎡1  0⎤       both directions get an equal step
               ⎣0  1⎦

Real momentum matrices have very low stable rank — a handful of singular values carry nearly all the mass, and the picture above is the typical case, not a contrived one. That is why flattening the spectrum buys real progress rather than just rescaling noise.

The geometric picture that ties all three together

Minimising a linear function over a convex set puts the optimum at an extreme point of that set. So the update always lands on a corner of the unit ball, and the three optimizers differ only in what the corners of their ball look like:

BallIts extreme pointsHence the update is
ℓ₂ — sphereevery boundary pointany direction: a scaled −g
ℓ∞ — cubethe 2d vertices, entries ±ra sign vector
spectralthe semi-orthogonal matrices (all σ equal)−r·UVᵀ
The one-sentence answer to "why does Muon output an orthogonal matrix?"

Because the orthogonal matrices are the corners of the spectral-norm ball — and a linear objective is always minimised at a corner. Nothing about orthogonality was designed in; it fell out of the choice of norm.

Why every optimizer acts one tensor at a time

A network's parameters are a tuple (W₁, …, W_L). Define the network-level norm as a max over layers of per-layer norms:

‖Δ‖ := maxℓ ‖Δℓ‖(ℓ)

Then ‖Δ‖ ≤ r holds exactly when ‖Δ_ℓ‖ ≤ r holds for every layer separately. The constraint set is a product, the objective is a sum, so the problem decouples and you dualise each tensor on its own. That is why every optimizer implementation is a loop over parameters — it is not an approximation, it is the exact solution under a max-type norm.

Two consequences worth having ready:

  • Choosing instead a sum-type norm (plain ℓ₂ over all parameters concatenated, which is what SGD does) couples every layer's step size to the global gradient norm. A layer with small gradients gets a small step forever, no matter how much progress is available there.
  • Because the per-layer norms are chosen independently, Muon-on-matrices + AdamW-on-everything-else is not a hack — it is a single well-defined mixed norm: spectral on the 2-D tensors, ℓ∞ on the 1-D ones.

Where momentum plugs in

All of the above is stated for the true gradient. You have a noisy minibatch sample, so substitute the momentum buffer for g and dualise that instead. Doing so converts the three cases into SGD-momentum, Adam, and Muon respectively.

Order matters: momentum then dualise

Muon buffers the raw gradients and orthogonalises the buffer — not the other way round. Orthogonalising first would throw away every matrix's magnitude before averaging, so the buffer would average a sequence of already-unit-norm matrices and lose the information momentum exists to accumulate. Momentum-first is also what Jordan found works better empirically, and it is what distinguishes Muon from earlier orthogonalising optimizers.

Which norm belongs to which tensor

The framework tells you the update given a norm. Choosing the norm is a modelling decision, and there is a principled version of it. Take a hidden layer W: ℝd_in → ℝd_out and measure vectors in RMS (activations in a normalised transformer are roughly RMS-controlled). For a vector with RMS(x) = 1 we have ‖x‖₂ = √d_in, so:

   ‖W‖_{RMS→RMS}  =  max  RMS(Wx)/RMS(x)  =  σ_max(W) · √(d_in/d_out)

   bounding the change in output RMS by r   ⇒   σ_max(ΔW) ≤ r·√(d_out/d_in)

   ⇒   ΔW  =  −η · √(d_out/d_in) · UVᵀ

There is the shape factor that appears in every Muon implementation. In PyTorch's torch.optim.Muon it is exposed as adjust_lr_fn: Keller Jordan's original scales the update by √max(1, A/B) (this derivation), while Moonshot's "match_rms_adamw" uses 0.2·√max(A, B) instead, which targets AdamW's empirical update RMS so you can transfer AdamW's tuned lr and wd directly.

The same reasoning explains the exclusion list. An embedding table is not applied to an RMS-normed vector — it is indexed by a one-hot, one row at a time. The RMS→RMS operator norm is simply the wrong measurement for it, so the spectral leash is the wrong leash, and AdamW's ℓ∞ geometry (which treats rows independently) is the right one. Likewise a 1-D gain or bias has no singular value spectrum to flatten. "Muon is for hidden layers" is a consequence of picking norms honestly, not a heuristic.

The whiteboard checklist

Five steps, any optimizer
  1. Linearise. L(w+Δ) ≈ L(w) + ⟨g, Δ⟩. Say out loud that this is unbounded below.
  2. Leash it. ‖Δ‖ ≤ r. State which norm, and why that norm suits this tensor.
  3. Bound it. ⟨g,Δ⟩ ≥ −r‖g‖★. Name the dual norm (ℓ₂→ℓ₂, ℓ∞→ℓ₁, spectral→nuclear).
  4. Achieve it. Write the Δ that makes the bound tight. That is your update direction: −g/‖g‖, −sign(g), −UVᵀ.
  5. Size it. Either keep r fixed (normalised update) or get it from a smoothness bound (η = 1/β, the unnormalised update).

Then bolt on the two engineering pieces: swap g for the momentum buffer, and add decoupled weight decay −ηλw outside the whole thing. You have just rebuilt SGD, Adam, AdamW and Muon without memorising a single update rule.

Sign conventions, since this is where whiteboards go wrong
  • Everything above minimises ⟨g,Δ⟩, so the answers carry a minus sign. If you define the LMO as a maximiser instead, put the minus in front.
  • The optimum value −r‖g‖★ is the predicted decrease in loss. A larger dual norm means more progress is available for the same step budget — which is a clean way to compare geometries.
  • r and η are not the same object. r is a distance in weight space; η is whatever your implementation multiplies the normalised direction by.

3. SGD (and momentum)

The updateMomentum, and the 1/(1−μ) trapNesterovProperties

The update

Row 1 of the norm table, with no smoothing at all:

w ← w − η · g

That's it. No state, no per-parameter adaptation. It is the baseline everything else is measured against, and on convolutional vision models it is still competitive with — often better-generalising than — anything adaptive.

Momentum, and the 1/(1−μ) trap

The gradient is noisy, so average it. PyTorch's convention:

   b ←  μ·b + g            μ = 0.9 typically
   w ←  w − η·b
This is a sum, not an average — and it changes your learning rate

Note the missing (1−μ). If the gradient were constant at g, the buffer converges to a geometric series:

b∞ = g · (1 + μ + μ² + …) = g / (1 − μ)

At μ = 0.9 that is a 10× larger effective step than plain SGD. Turning on momentum without dropping the learning rate roughly tenfold is a classic way to blow up a run. The other convention, b ← μ·b + (1−μ)·g (a true EMA, what Adam uses for m), has b∞ = g and no such gain. Know which one your framework uses.

The second reason momentum helps, beyond variance reduction: in a narrow ravine the oscillating components cancel across steps while the consistent downhill component accumulates. You get a larger step along the valley floor and a smaller one across it, for free.

Nesterov

Evaluate the gradient at the point momentum is about to carry you to, rather than where you are — a look-ahead correction. In practice PyTorch implements it as using g + μ·b in place of b. Small, usually-positive effect; not worth much interview time except to know it is a look-ahead, not a different geometry.

4. Adam

What it fixesThe updateBias correction, derivedWhy divide by √vAdam is smoothed sign descentHyperparameters

What it fixes

SGD has two separate problems, and Adam attacks both with one buffer each:

  • Noise — a minibatch gradient is a high-variance estimate. Fix: EMA of g. That is m, the first moment.
  • Scale heterogeneity — different parameters have gradients of wildly different magnitude, so no single η fits all. Fix: divide each coordinate by an estimate of its own gradient magnitude. That is v, the second moment, used as an RMS.

The update

   m ←  β₁·m + (1−β₁)·g          EMA of the gradient          (β₁ = 0.9)
   v ←  β₂·v + (1−β₂)·g²         EMA of the squared gradient  (β₂ = 0.95…0.999)

   m̂ = m / (1 − β₁ᵗ)             bias correction
   v̂ = v / (1 − β₂ᵗ)

   w ←  w − η · m̂ / (√v̂ + ε)

Read the last line as: direction from m̂, magnitude normalised away by √v̂. Adam decides where to go from the first moment and refuses to let the second moment decide how far.

Bias correction, derived

Both buffers start at zero, so early on they are biased toward zero. Unroll the recursion:

mt = (1−β₁) · Σi=1..t β₁t−i gi

If every gradient were the same value g, the coefficients sum to (1−β₁)(1−β₁ᵗ)/(1−β₁) = 1−β₁ᵗ. So mt = (1−β₁ᵗ)·g — short by exactly that factor. Divide it out. Same argument for v.

Note — what happens if you forget it

At t = 1 with β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999: m₁ = 0.1g and v₁ = 0.001g², so the uncorrected ratio is

m₁ / √v₁ = 0.1·g / (0.0316·|g|) ≈ 3.2 · sign(g)

The first steps are about 3× too large, not too small — the correction to v (a square root of a much smaller number) dominates the correction to m. In general the early overshoot factor is (1−β₁)/√(1−β₂). This is exactly the regime warmup also protects you from.

Why divide by √v — the units argument

Multiply your loss by a constant c. Then g → c·g, so m → c·m and √v → c·√v, and the ratio m̂/√v̂ is unchanged. Adam's step size is invariant to the scale of the gradient — globally, and per-coordinate.

That single property is why Adam works on transformers and SGD struggles: every layer effectively gets its own learning rate, discovered automatically. It is also why Adam is indifferent to loss scaling in mixed precision (as long as you unscale consistently), and why m̂/√v̂ is dimensionless — η is measured in units of weights, not units of gradient.

ε breaks the invariance — on purpose, and sometimes by accident

ε is a floor, so the invariance only holds while √v̂ ≫ ε. If gradients are small relative to ε, the denominator becomes roughly constant and Adam degenerates into SGD with learning rate η/ε. Also note the placement: PyTorch uses √v̂ + ε (outside the root), some papers use √(v̂ + ε) (inside). They are not the same, and people have chased phantom bugs over it.

Adam is smoothed sign descent

Set β₁ = β₂ and suppose the gradient is roughly steady. Then v ≈ g², so

m̂ / √v̂ ≈ g / |g| = sign(g)

which is row 2 of the norm table: steepest descent under ℓ∞. Adam is signSGD with two EMAs bolted on. This gives you the useful mental picture that the normalised update has magnitude near 1 in every coordinate — in practice its RMS lands around 0.2–0.4, a number that matters later for Muon's scaling.

Cost: 2 states × 4 bytes = 8 bytes/param. FLOPs are trivial; the step is memory-bandwidth bound, which is why foreach / fused implementations are meaningfully faster.

5. AdamW

The bug it fixesThe updateDecay is coupled to the LRIn practice

The bug it fixes

There are two things people call "weight decay", and they are only the same thing under SGD.

   L2 penalty         add  ½λ‖w‖²  to the loss   →  g̃ = g + λ·w
   weight decay       shrink the weight directly →  w ← (1 − ηλ)·w

Under SGD they coincide exactly: w − η(g + λw) = w − ηg − ηλw. Nobody noticed a difference for thirty years. Under Adam they do not, because the L2 term goes through the preconditioner:

Adam + L2: Δ ∝ ( m of (g + λw) ) / √( v of (g + λw) )

The λw contribution gets divided by √v. So a parameter with a large gradient history has a large √v and therefore receives less regularisation — the exact opposite of what you want, and completely unintended. Regularisation strength ends up coupled to gradient history.

The update

AdamW simply refuses to let decay through the preconditioner:

   m ←  β₁·m + (1−β₁)·g
   v ←  β₂·v + (1−β₂)·g²

   w ←  w − η · m̂/(√v̂ + ε)  −  η·λ·w
                ▲                  ▲
         adaptive part      untouched by v — that's the whole idea

Everything about Adam is unchanged. One term moved out of the fraction. That is the entire paper (Loshchilov & Hutter, "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization").

Decay is still coupled to the learning rate

The follow-on trap

In the standard implementation the decay term carries an η. So the shrink per step is η·λ, and it follows your learning-rate schedule: as cosine decay drives η → 0, the effective regularisation vanishes too. Two consequences worth stating in an interview:

  • Changing lr silently changes how strongly you regularise. The quantity that transfers across runs is lr · wd, not wd.
  • Weight norms reach an equilibrium where per-step shrinkage balances per-step growth from the update. Weight decay in modern LLM training is better understood as implicit control of weight/update norms than as classical capacity control.

The idea

MomentUm Orthogonalized by Newton–Schulz (Jordan et al., 2024). Adam treats a weight matrix as a bag of unrelated scalars. But a hidden weight matrix is a linear map: what matters is how much it stretches each input direction, i.e. its singular values. So put the leash on the spectral norm — row 3 of the norm table.

argmax‖Δ‖spectral ≤ 1 ⟨G, Δ⟩ = UVᵀ, where G = UΣVᵀ

In words: take the momentum matrix, keep its singular vectors, throw away its singular values, set them all to 1. Same directions, equalised magnitudes. Jordan states the same operation a second way, which is worth knowing because it makes the "post-processing step" framing obvious:

Ortho(G) = argminO { ‖O − G‖F : OᵀO = I or OOᵀ = I }

— the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix to G. Steepest descent under the spectral norm and "snap the update to the closest orthogonal matrix" are the same computation arrived at from two directions.

Note — why this is a good idea empirically

Jordan's stated motivation is observational: the update matrices produced by SGD-momentum and by Adam for 2-D transformer parameters typically have very high condition number — nearly low-rank, with a few directions dominating every neuron's update. Orthogonalising raises the scale of the "rare directions", which are small in the update but still matter for learning. Same reasoning as Adam's per-coordinate normalisation, lifted from coordinates to singular directions.

The core algorithm

This is Muon as stated in the papers' algorithm boxes — the object of study, stripped of everything practical:

   B_t ←  μ·B_{t-1} + G_t             plain heavy-ball momentum,  μ = 0.95
   O_t  =  NewtonSchulz5(B_t)  ≈ UVᵀ  orthogonalise
   θ_t ←  θ_{t-1} − η·O_t

Note what is not there: no second moment (Muon carries one buffer, like SGD-momentum), and also no Nesterov, no weight decay, and no shape-dependent scaling. Those three are exactly what the shipped implementations add — see below.

Newton–Schulz — why a polynomial, and why these numbers

An exact SVD per matrix per step would be far too slow, and the coupled Newton iteration used in Shampoo implementations needs fp32 to stay stable. Newton–Schulz survives bf16, which is why it was chosen.

What "matrix polynomial" means, and why odd powers

For a scalar, φ(x) = ax + bx³ + cx⁵ is obvious. For a matrix it is not, for two reasons. You cannot just cube the entries — that would be elementwise, and would destroy the linear-map structure you care about. And for a rectangular G of shape m×n, G³ is not even defined; the shapes do not compose.

The fix is the sandwich form. These three are all m×n, so they can be added:

   G          (GGᵀ)G          (GGᵀ)²G          ← "x", "x³", "x⁵"

   every term is  G  wrapped in an even number of extra copies of itself,
   so the shape always comes back to m×n

Hence only odd powers. There is a second reason to want oddness beyond shape bookkeeping: an odd φ satisfies φ(−x) = −φ(x), so Ortho(−G) = −Ortho(G). Flip the sign of the gradient and the update flips too — which you obviously want from an optimizer.

Note — the mental model: a scalar function applied to the spectrum

The point of the algebra below is that a matrix polynomial is just a scalar function applied to the singular values, computed with nothing but matmuls. U and V never move. You are editing the spectrum of a matrix without ever computing its SVD.

The closest scalar analogy is Newton's method for a reciprocal:

x ← x·(2 − a·x) converges to 1/a using only multiplication — no division

Same spirit, and the same lineage: replace an expensive primitive (division there, SVD here) with a handful of cheap ones. That is exactly what the name Newton–Schulz refers to.

The mechanism is a one-line calculation. With G = UΣVᵀ, one iteration of the odd matrix polynomial gives:

   G' = aG + b(GGᵀ)G + c(GGᵀ)²G
      = (aI + bUΣ²Uᵀ + cUΣ⁴Uᵀ)·UΣVᵀ
      = U·(aΣ + bΣ³ + cΣ⁵)·Vᵀ

   so N steps give   U·φᴺ(Σ)·Vᵀ,   where  φ(x) = ax + bx³ + cx⁵
                                    applied elementwise to the singular values

U and V never move. The iteration only reshapes the spectrum. So convergence to UVᵀ needs exactly two things:

  1. Start with all σ in [0, 1] — achieved by dividing G by ‖G‖F first. Harmless, since Ortho(cG) = Ortho(G).
  2. Pick (a,b,c) so that φᴺ(x) → 1 on [0, 1]. Many choices work; the naive (2, −1.5, 0.5) is fine.

Choosing the coefficients

Everything so far says what the polynomial has to do. It has to satisfy exactly one correctness condition:

φᴺ(x) → 1 for every x ∈ (0, 1]

Because if every singular value goes to 1, then U·φᴺ(Σ)·Vᵀ → U·I·Vᵀ = UVᵀ, which is the orthogonalisation we wanted. (φᴺ means φ composed with itself N times, not the Nth power.)

That condition does not pin down (a, b, c) — it leaves a whole family. The naive (2, −1.5, 0.5) satisfies it perfectly well. So correctness is not the design problem. Speed is: every extra iteration costs another few matmuls on every 2-D parameter on every step, so the real objective is to reach the target in as few iterations as possible. The coefficients are chosen to minimise iteration count, not to minimise error.

Consideration 1 — make a as large as possible

Differentiate: φ'(x) = a + 3bx² + 5cx⁴, so φ'(0) = a. Near zero the cubic and quintic terms vanish and φ(x) ≈ ax — each iteration simply multiplies a small singular value by roughly a. Growth is geometric, which gives a closed form for the bottleneck:

iterations to lift σ₀ up to ≈1 ≈ log(1/σ₀) / log(a)

And small singular values are the bottleneck. After dividing by ‖B‖F, a matrix of effective rank r has singular values sitting near 1/√r with a long tail below that — so most of the spectrum starts small, and the whole job is dragging it up. a is the single number governing that rate:

Starting σ₀Steps with a = 3.4445 (measured / formula)Steps with a = 2.0 (measured / formula)
0.014 / 3.77 / 6.6
0.0016 / 5.611 / 10.0
0.00018 / 7.414 / 13.3

The ratio is log(3.4445)/log(2) ≈ 1.78 — a bigger a buys you nearly half the iterations, uniformly, at every starting scale. That is the entire reason anyone bothered tuning these numbers.

Consideration 2 — but it still has to land near 1

You cannot simply crank a up: the polynomial has to remain well-behaved on all of [0, 1], and a steeper slope at the origin means a wilder polynomial further out. So the constraint is that the iterates must end up close to 1:

limN→∞ φᴺ(x) ∈ [1 − ε, 1 + ε] for all x ∈ (0, 1]

The surprising empirical finding is how loose ε is allowed to be. ε ≈ 0.3 does not harm the loss curve for Muon trainings — the update can be 30% off from a true orthogonal matrix and training does not notice. That single observation is what makes the whole approach cheap, because it converts a convergence requirement into a much weaker "get in the neighbourhood" requirement.

The resulting problem
   maximise    a
   subject to  lim φᴺ(x) ∈ [0.7, 1.3]   for all x ∈ (0, 1]

Solved with an ad-hoc gradient-based search, this yields (3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315), at which point 5 iterations suffice for transformer language models and small convnets. Third- and seventh-order polynomials were also tried; neither improved the wallclock further, so the quintic stayed.

Note — how to read the figure, and the trap in it

Plot φ, φ⁵, ρ, ρ⁵ on [0, 1] (ρ = the tuned polynomial) and the objective and its cost are both visible at once:

  • The objective — ρ's slope at the origin is 3.4445 against φ's 2.0. That gap is φ'(0) = a, the quantity being maximised.
  • The cost — ρ overshoots to ≈1.2 near x = 0.55 and falls to 0.701 at x = 1. No fixed point at 1, which is why ρ⁵ never flattens and instead chops through the band forever.

The trap: over most of the domain the naive polynomial looks strictly better. φ⁵ is a clean flat 1 from about x = 0.05 rightwards; ρ⁵ is visibly ragged everywhere. On that picture you would pick the naive coefficients.

The visible half of the plot is the half that does not matter. Zoom into the left edge and it inverts:

After Frobenius normalisation Σσᵢ² = 1, so a matrix of effective rank r has singular values near 1/√r with a long tail beneath. The naive polynomial is beautifully accurate on the directions that were already fine and does nothing for the ones you cared about. The tuned polynomial is sloppy everywhere, but sloppy inside the ±0.3 band that provably does not matter, and it actually reaches the tail.

The shipped polynomial does not converge at all — and that is the design

Iterate the two coefficient sets by hand and the difference is stark. The naive (2, −1.5, 0.5) has φ(1) = 1 and φ'(1) = 0: a textbook superattracting fixed point, quadratic convergence. The tuned coefficients have φ(1) = 0.701 — 1 is not a fixed point. Starting from 1.0 the orbit is:

   1.000 → 0.701 → 1.114 → 0.721 → 1.090 → 0.696 → 1.119 → 0.728 → …

   a 2-cycle bouncing around 1 forever, never settling

So why ship it? Because after Frobenius normalisation the singular values of a real momentum matrix are small — for a matrix with effective rank r they sit near 1/√r, and the tail is far below that. Lifting the tail is the whole job, and φ'(0) = a is what governs that rate:

The trade is exact convergence for a steeper slope at zero. You give up ever landing on 1 and accept an orbit inside [0.7, 1.3]; in exchange the singular values that actually needed help get there in roughly half the steps. And the sloppiness is harmless because approximate orthogonalisation is still steepest descent under a Schatten-p norm for large finite p, which is indistinguishable from the spectral norm in practice.

python — Jordan's NS5
def newtonschulz5(G, steps=5, eps=1e-7):
    a, b, c = (3.4445, -4.7750, 2.0315)
    X = G.bfloat16()
    X /= (X.norm() + eps)              # put all singular values in [0, 1]
    if G.size(0) > G.size(1):
        X = X.T                        # work with the smaller Gram matrix
    for _ in range(steps):
        A = X @ X.T
        B = b * A + c * A @ A          # factored form: 4nm² + 2m³, not 6nm²
        X = a * X + B @ X
    if G.size(0) > G.size(1):
        X = X.T
    return X

Two implementation details in there are load-bearing: the transpose so the Gram matrix is formed on the smaller dimension, and the factored B @ X rather than expanding the polynomial term by term.

The two scaling conventions

An exactly orthogonal O of shape A×B has all min(A,B) singular values equal to 1, so ‖O‖F = √min(A,B) and:

RMS(O) = ‖O‖F / √(A·B) = √min(A,B) / √(A·B) = 1 / √max(A,B)

The update's typical size therefore depends on the matrix's shape, which is not something you want. Two different fixes are in circulation, and torch.optim.Muon ships both:

FactorWhat it targets
"original" — Jordan (PyTorch default)√max(1, A/B)The RMS→RMS operator-norm derivation: √(d_out/d_in), clamped at 1. Makes shapes consistent with each other.
"match_rms_adamw" — Moonshot0.2·√max(A,B)Drives RMS(update) to ≈ 0.2, AdamW's empirical update RMS, so AdamW's tuned lr and wd transfer directly.
These are not interchangeable

They differ by more than a constant — one is √(A/B), the other √max(A,B). If you port a learning rate between codebases without checking which convention is in force, you can be off by a factor of the model dimension's square root. PyTorch defaults to "original", so an AdamW-tuned lr will not transfer unless you explicitly ask for "match_rms_adamw".

Scope — what it does not cover

Muon is a hidden-layer optimizer, not a whole-model optimizer

It applies to 2-D hidden weight matrices only. Scalars, vectors, and — empirically important — the input embedding and the final classifier head go to AdamW, even though those two are 2-D. For the embedding this follows from modular-norm theory (its input is a one-hot, not an RMS-normed vector, so the spectral leash measures the wrong thing); for the output head, Jordan notes it is driven by empirics rather than theory. Convolutional kernels work by flattening the last three dimensions.

Note — the QKV detail

Muon works better applied to Q, K and V separately than to a fused QKV matrix, which is how most transformer implementations parametrise them. Makes sense under the norm story: a fused QKV is three different linear maps stacked, and orthogonalising the stack is not the same as orthogonalising each. Easy to get wrong and rarely mentioned.

At scale

Muon went from a NanoGPT speedrun trick to frontier pretraining in about a year. Two changes made it scale — and they are exactly the two the algorithm box omits:

  • Decoupled weight decay — vanilla Muon lets weight norms grow without bound over long runs.
  • Consistent update RMS — the 0.2·√max(A,B) convention above.

With those, Moonshot's Moonlight report claimed roughly 2× token efficiency versus AdamW at matched compute. Kimi K2 (1T-parameter MoE) pretrained on 15.5T tokens with MuonClip = Muon + weight decay + RMS matching + QK-Clip, which rescales the query/key projections whenever the maximum attention logit exceeds a threshold τ ≈ 100 — a targeted fix for the attention-logit explosion that Muon triggers more readily than AdamW does.

Worth noting for context: as of the original blog post, "will Muon scale to 20B+ parameters and 1T+ tokens?" and "can the NS iterations be properly distributed across a large cluster?" were listed as open questions. The first has since been answered affirmatively; the second is still where the engineering pain lives.

Systems friction — the thing interviewers probe

Orthogonalisation needs the whole matrix

Newton–Schulz is a global operation on B. Under ZeRO-2/3 or FSDP the momentum matrix is sharded across ranks, so you must gather it, orthogonalise, then scatter. "Distributed Muon" implementations assign whole matrices to ranks, compute locally, and broadcast the result. Adam has no such problem — it is purely elementwise, so it shards perfectly. This is the main reason Muon is not a drop-in replacement in an existing sharded training stack.

Lineage worth one sentence

Strip the preconditioner accumulation out of Shampoo and its update collapses algebraically to UVᵀ — the orthogonalised gradient. Add momentum before the orthogonalisation and you have Muon. So Muon is fairly described as "accumulation-free Shampoo, computed with Newton–Schulz instead of inverse fourth roots". Earlier orthogonalisation-based methods (Orthogonal-SGDM, stochastic spectral descent) either applied momentum after orthogonalising or had none at all — momentum-first is the empirical difference.

Note — two rows that need care attributing
  • BF16 moments. The recipe is real and lands at 12. What the table is really telling you

Reading down the totals: 16 → 20 → 12 → 10 → 6 → 5. 8-bit optimizers, in one paragraph

Naively casting m and v to int8 destroys them: a single outlier in a tensor sets the scale and every other value quantises to zero. Two ideas fix it (Dettmers et al.):

  • Block-wise quantisation — split the tensor into blocks of ~2048 and give each block its own normalisation constant, so one outlier can only ruin its own block. It also makes quantisation embarrassingly parallel.
  • Dynamic exponent quantisation — a non-linear codebook that spends bits on relative rather than absolute precision, matching the wide dynamic range that optimizer states actually have.

Result: 8 → 2 bytes/param of state, with pretraining and fine-tuning quality matching 32-bit. It is a one-line change (bnb.optim.AdamW8bit), which makes it the standard answer to "you are 20 GB over budget".

Note — how these compose

They stack, and the ordering of what to reach for is roughly: shard first (lossless), quantise second (cheap, near-lossless), offload third (costs throughput), change the algorithm last (costs retuning). Also remember activations are a separate budget entirely — activation checkpointing is orthogonal to everything on this page.

The paper algorithm box has no Nesterov, no weight decay and no s. Every shipped implementation has all three.

Thirteen traps

#Trap
1Adam(weight_decay=…) is L2, not decoupled. Use AdamW.
2PyTorch SGD momentum is a sum, not an EMA → effective step is 1/(1−μ) = 10× larger.
3AdamW's effective decay is η·λ and follows the LR schedule. lr·wd is what transfers.
4Without bias correction the first steps are (1−β₁)/√(1−β₂) ≈ 3.2× too large.
5Warmup is not optional for Adam: v̂ is a bad estimate for the first ~1/(1−β₂) steps.
6√v̂ + ε ≠ √(v̂ + ε). And an ε that is too large silently turns Adam into SGD.
7Never decay biases or LayerNorm/RMSNorm gains; often skip embeddings too.
8Adam is invariant to gradient scale; SGD is not. This is why Adam handles transformers' wildly differing per-layer gradient magnitudes.
9Muon covers 2-D hidden weights only. Embeddings + LM head + 1-D params → AdamW.
10Muon needs the full matrix to orthogonalise → gather/scatter under FSDP or ZeRO-2/3. Adam shards trivially because it is elementwise.
11β₂ sets an averaging window of 1/(1−β₂) steps. LLM runs use 0.95 (~20 steps) so v recovers quickly after a loss spike.
12LR–batch scaling: roughly linear for SGD, roughly √ for Adam (the update is already normalised).
13Gradient clipping happens before the optimizer. With Adam its main job is stopping a spike from poisoning v for the next ~1/(1−β₂) steps.
16Mixed precision does not always save memory. Megatron's default FP32 gradient accumulation makes the real figure 20 bytes/param, not 16 — the usual cause of an OOM that your arithmetic said would fit.
17Moments are safe to demote to BF16 or 8-bit; accumulators are not. DeepSeek-V3 moved the AdamW moments to BF16 but kept master weights and gradients in FP32 precisely because the gradient is the batch-accumulation buffer.
14Muon's shape-scaling convention is not standard. √max(1, A/B) (Jordan, and PyTorch's default) and 0.2·√max(A,B) (Moonshot) differ by more than a constant. An AdamW-tuned lr only transfers under the second.
15The Muon in a paper's algorithm box ≠ the Muon you run. Boxes typically omit Nesterov, decoupled weight decay, and shape scaling — the last two being exactly what made it scale.

The 60-second drill

Say this out loud before the interview
  1. Linearise the loss, constrain the step in some norm, solve. The norm is the optimizer: ℓ₂ → SGD, ℓ∞ → sign/Adam, spectral → Muon.
  2. Momentum is an EMA over 1/(1−β) steps. It reduces variance; PyTorch's SGD version also multiplies your step by 1/(1−μ).
  3. Adam = direction from m, magnitude normalised by √v. That ratio is scale-invariant, which is the whole reason it works on transformers. Bias-correct both because they start at zero.
  4. AdamW = move weight decay outside the √v fraction. Identical to Adam otherwise; identical to L2 under SGD.
  5. Muon = momentum, then flatten the matrix's singular values to 1 via a 5-step Newton–Schulz polynomial in bf16 — equivalently, snap the update to the nearest semi-orthogonal matrix. Then a shape-dependent scale factor whose convention you must check. One state, not two. Hidden 2-D weights only; FLOP overhead ≈ T·m/B, under 1%.
  6. 16 bytes/param for AdamW mixed precision; 12 of them are shardable optimizer-side state. Shard, then quantise to 8-bit, then offload, then change algorithm.
