The whole document in four sentences

A generative model can be an ODE: start at noise, integrate a learned velocity field, land on data. CNFs (2018) trained this by maximum likelihood, which forces you to simulate the ODE and a Jacobian-trace integral inside every training step — that is why they never scaled. Flow matching (2023) notices you can choose the path yourself: draw noise ε and data z, put the model on the straight line between them, and regress its output onto the line's known velocity z − ε. Training becomes one network eval and an MSE — simulation-free — and simulation is only paid once, at sampling time.

Scalar field: one number per point

A scalar field assigns one plain number to every point in space: f : ℝ^d → ℝ. Temperature on a weather map; altitude on a hiking map; a loss surface f(θ) over parameters. A number has a size but no direction.

Vector field: one arrow per point

A vector field assigns a vector to every point: u : ℝ^d → ℝ^d. A vector is an arrow, and an arrow carries two pieces of information: a direction (which way) and a magnitude (how strongly). Wind on a weather map is the canonical picture. So yes — vector fields have directions; that is precisely what separates them from scalar fields:

   scalar field  f(x,y) = x² + y²             vector field  u(x,y) = [x, y]
   (one NUMBER per point)                     (one ARROW per point)

        8   5   4   5   8                          ↖    ↑    ↗
        5   2   1   2   5                          ←    ·    →
        4   1   0   1   4                          ↙    ↓    ↘

   size only, no direction                    direction AND magnitude —
                                              arrows point AWAY from the origin,
                                              longer the farther out you are

The two are related: every scalar field induces a vector field through its gradient — [x, y] above is literally ∇(½(x²+y²)), the steepest-ascent arrows of the bowl on the left. (Gradient descent follows −∇f; diffusion’s score ∇log p in tab 5 is another gradient field.) In this document the arrow is always a velocity: direction = where a particle sitting at x should head, magnitude = how fast. The running example of tabs 1–2 is the inward version of the picture above:

   u_t(x) = −x drawn on the 1-D line — every point gets an arrow toward 0:

   x:     −3      −2      −1       0       +1      +2      +3
   u:    ───▶    ──▶     ─▶       ·       ◀─      ◀──     ◀───
   direction: toward the origin         magnitude: |x| — stronger far away

Time-dependent vector field: a movie of arrow pictures

Add time as an input: u : ℝ^d × [0,1] → ℝ^d, written u_t(x). Equivalently, a whole family of static vector fields, one per instant — a movie whose every frame is an arrow picture. A time-independent (autonomous) field is the movie whose frames are all identical; u_t(x) = −x is one. A genuinely time-dependent example: u_t(x) = −t·x — at t = 0 the field is zero everywhere (nothing moves), and the inward pull strengthens as t grows. The one that matters later, CondOT’s u_t(x|z) = (z−x)/(1−t), is time-dependent in the same sense: the same point x gets a faster arrow as the deadline t = 1 approaches.

Note — how do you draw a field that depends on t?

A single arrow plot freezes one instant, so a genuinely time-dependent field needs one of three devices. (1) Filmstrip: side-by-side snapshots of the arrows at t = 0, 0.5, 1. (2) Animation: redraw the arrows every frame as t advances — tab 1’s ODE playground has a deadline field u_t(x) = (z*−x)/(1−t) drawn exactly this way: the arrow grid itself grows as t → 1, then the run resets. (3) Space–time plot (for 1-D state, and the most useful picture in this document): put t on the horizontal axis and x on the vertical. Then u_t(x) is a slope attached to every point (t, x) of the plane, and trajectories are curves that follow the slopes. Tab 3’s interactive is precisely this picture — its straight lines and magenta curves are trajectories drawn in space–time, and dragging the t slider sweeps a vertical "now" line across it.

What an ODE actually is

An ordinary differential equation is an equation whose unknown is a function, and which constrains that function through its derivative. ("Ordinary" = derivatives in one variable only — time, for us; the continuity equation, with its ∂/∂x’s, is a partial DE.) Everything in this document is the first-order case with a starting point pinned down — an initial value problem:

dX_t/dt = u_t(X_t),    X_0 = x_0

Unpack it: the unknown is the whole trajectory X (a function of t, not a number); the equation demands that at every instant, the trajectory’s velocity equals the field’s arrow at its current position (derivative = instantaneous velocity is the entire dictionary between calculus and motion); and the initial condition selects which of the many solutions you mean. "Solving" = exhibiting a function that satisfies both.

Note — existence & uniqueness, and why we care

If u is continuously differentiable with bounded derivatives (a neural network qualifies), then through every starting point there passes exactly one trajectory. Two consequences get used later: trajectories can never cross (the reason tab 3’s marginal curves bend around each other instead of intersecting), and the flow can be run backwards uniquely — it is invertible, which is what tab 2’s CNFs cash in for likelihoods.

   what goes wrong without the conditions:   dx/dt = x² ,  x(0) = x₀ > 0
   separate and integrate:   x(t) = x₀ / (1 − x₀·t)
   → BLOWS UP to ∞ at the finite time t = 1/x₀
   (superlinear growth lets a particle reach infinity before the clock runs out)

So the checklist a field must satisfy for the theory in this document to hold: (i) defined on the whole domain, at every t; (ii) continuous; (iii) Lipschitz in x — |u(x)−u(y)| ≤ L|x−y| — or C¹ with bounded derivatives, the condition doing the real work (existence and uniqueness); (iv) at-most-linear growth, so no finite-time blow-up; (v) differentiable wherever the trace tr(∂u/∂x) is needed (tab 2). One FM-specific footnote: CondOT’s (z−x)/(1−t) passes all of these for each fixed t < 1, but its Lipschitz constant is exactly 1/(1−t), which diverges at the endpoint — the concrete reason samplers integrate to t = 1−ε (tab 3’s demo stops at 0.995) and the original FM paper keeps a σ_min floor.

Solving one by hand (the only hand-solve you’ll ever need here)

   dx/dt = −x ,  x(0) = 2                       (separable — put x left, t right)

   dx / x   = −dt
   ∫ both:    ln|x| = −t + C
   exponentiate:  x(t) = A·e^{−t}         initial condition:  x(0) = 2  ⇒  A = 2
   ALWAYS verify:  d/dt [2e^{−t}] = −2e^{−t} = −x(t)  ✓   and  x(0) = 2  ✓

When there is no formula: a 10-line solver

Go back to the definition of the derivative: for small h, x(t+h) ≈ x(t) + h·x'(t). The ODE tells you what x'(t) is — the field’s arrow. Substituting it in is Euler’s method:

generic Euler solver + convergence check
def euler(u, x0, t0=0.0, t1=1.0, steps=100):
    x, t = x0, t0
    h = (t1 - t0) / steps
    for _ in range(steps):
        x = x + h * u(x, t)      # the one line that matters
        t = t + h
    return x

# check on dx/dt = -x, x0 = 2.   exact answer: 2·e^{-1} = 0.7358
print(euler(lambda x, t: -x, 2.0, steps=10))     # 0.6974   coarse
print(euler(lambda x, t: -x, 2.0, steps=100))    # 0.7321
print(euler(lambda x, t: -x, 2.0, steps=1000))   # 0.7354   converging ~ O(h)

def midpoint(u, x0, t0=0.0, t1=1.0, steps=100):      # what Voicebox uses
    x, t = x0, t0
    h = (t1 - t0) / steps
    for _ in range(steps):
        k1 = u(x, t)                                 # arrow at the start
        k2 = u(x + 0.5 * h * k1, t + 0.5 * h)        # arrow at the midpoint
        x, t = x + h * k2, t + h                     # full step with THAT slope
    return x                                         # 2 evals/step, error O(h^2)

print(midpoint(lambda x, t: -x, 2.0, steps=10))  # 0.7371 — 10 steps beat
print(euler(lambda x, t: -x, 2.0, steps=20))     # 0.7169    Euler's 20 (same NFE!)

Three remarks that cover most solver questions: the error of Euler shrinks like O(h) (halve the step, halve the error); midpoint spends a second field evaluation per step to probe the direction halfway and gets O(h²) (this is what Voicebox uses); and every fancier solver — Runge–Kutta, adaptive — is still just "evaluate u, take a step" with cleverer bookkeeping. For a neural field each evaluation is a forward pass, which is why solver cost is counted in NFE, the number of function evaluations. Tab 1’s Euler section shows the same arithmetic with numbers you can do in your head.

How the initial condition enters Euler: it is the recursion’s base case — the seed everything unrolls from, and the point where the very first arrow is read:

   X⁽⁰⁾ = X₀ = 2                          ← the seed; the FIRST field evaluation
   X⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = X⁽ᵏ⁾ + h·u_{t_k}(X⁽ᵏ⁾),  t_k = k·h      happens AT the initial condition

   unrolled on u = −x (each step multiplies by 1−h) — X₀ factors out:
   X⁽ᵏ⁾ = (1−h)ᵏ · X₀          vs exact          ψ_{t_k}(X₀) = e^{−t_k} · X₀
   and (1−h)^{t/h} → e^{−t} as h → 0.

   in general:  X⁽ⁿ⁾ = (I + h·u_{t_{n−1}}) ∘ … ∘ (I + h·u_{t_0}) (X₀)
   — the composed step maps ARE a discrete flow map; X₀ is its input.
   demo settings h = 0.3, t = 3:  (0.7)¹⁰·2 = 0.0565  vs  2e⁻³ = 0.0996
   — the 0.043 gap is exactly the |error| in the readout below.

The step up from Euler, and the solver Voicebox actually ships — explicit midpoint (RK2). Euler’s flaw is systematic: it reads the slope at the start of the interval and pretends it holds for all of h. Midpoint spends a second evaluation to probe half a step ahead and uses the arrow there — a centered slope that cancels the first-order error term:

   k₁ = u_{t_k}(X⁽ᵏ⁾)                        ← probe: arrow at the start
   X_mid = X⁽ᵏ⁾ + (h/2)·k₁                   ← walk HALF a step
   k₂ = u_{t_k + h/2}(X_mid)                  ← read the arrow at the midpoint
   X⁽ᵏ⁺¹⁾ = X⁽ᵏ⁾ + h·k₂                     ← take the FULL step with that slope

   2 evals/step · global error O(h²): halve h ⇒ QUARTER the error (Euler: halve)

   unrolled on u = −x — the per-step multiplier is a Taylor series of e⁻ʰ:
   Euler:    1 − h              = e⁻ʰ to 1st order
   midpoint: 1 − h + h²/2       = e⁻ʰ to 2nd order

   fair comparison at EQUAL NFE = 20 evals, t: 0 → 1, x₀ = 2 (exact 0.7358):
   Euler,   20 steps:  0.7169   |error| = 0.019
   midpoint, 10 steps: 0.7371   |error| = 0.0013     ≈ 14× better, same compute

That equal-NFE column is the whole argument for higher-order solvers on smooth fields — and the reason Voicebox uses fixed-step midpoint (via torchdiffeq). One accounting caveat from tab 6: under classifier-free guidance each "evaluation of u" is itself two network passes.

Semantics first: one motion, two languages

(Primitives — field types, ODE mechanics, Euler — are tab 0.) The sentence to build everything from: the flow takes a time t and a starting position x_0 and outputs a destination point — but that destination is reached with some dynamics: the particle moves with a certain velocity at every instant along the way, and that velocity is exactly what the vector field defines. Same motion, two languages — the field speaks velocities, the flow speaks positions. Their type signatures are identical (ℝ^d × [0,1] → ℝ^d); only the semantics separates them:

                        u_t(x)  — the field           ψ_t(x₀)  — the flow
   output means         a VELOCITY (arrow at x)       a POSITION (a new point)
   units                distance / time               distance
   question answered    "which way, how fast,         "where has the particle
                         right now, here?"             from x₀ ended up by t?"
   role                 derivative · local ·          integral · global ·
                        instantaneous instruction     accumulated result
   check at t = 0       u₀(2) = −2 — just an arrow      ψ₀(2) = 2 — nobody has moved yet
   on the example       u(2) = −2   (a rate)          ψ₁(2) = 2e⁻¹ = 0.736  (a place)
   from the other       differentiate the flow        integrate the field (solve ODE)

One function, three slices

  • fix nothing → the flow — everything at once: it defines the trajectories for all starting positions x_0 and all times t.
  • fix t → ψ_t(·) : ℝ^d → ℝ^d, a map of space — every point moved simultaneously, a warp. (The displacement arrows x_0 ↦ ψ_t(x_0)−x_0 are a vector field — but the accumulated one, not the instantaneous u_t.)
  • fix x_0 → ψ_·(x_0) : [0,1] → ℝ^d, the trajectory of the particle defined by its starting position x_0 — one curve; input is time, so not a field. The trajectory is pinned by the start: one start = one trajectory, so two particles cannot share a starting position.
  • differentiate the trajectory in t → velocities, but only along that one curve — do it for every x_0 and you recover the full field u_t everywhere.
   the slices, on  ψ_t(x₀) = e⁻ᵗ·x₀ :
   fix t = 0.5     ψ₀.₅(·) : x₀ ↦ 0.607·x₀     a warp — every point moved at once
   fix x₀ = 2      ψ_·(2)  : t ↦ 2e⁻ᵗ          a trajectory — input is TIME
   d/dt of it                t ↦ −2e⁻ᵗ          velocities along that one curve
   … for every x₀           u_t(x) = −x           the field, recovered everywhere

Can two particles collide later — ψ_{t′}(x_0) = ψ_{t′}(x_1) with x_0 ≠ x_1? No. The note below does it in equations; headline: the gap between two trajectories shrinks at most exponentially and can never reach zero in finite time. Hence each ψ_{t′} is a bijection of space (the invertibility tab 2’s CNFs cash in). What is allowed: the same place at different times — under e^{−t}x_0, position 1 is visited by bib 2 at t = ln 2 and by bib 4 at t = ln 4. Same place, different clock readings, no collision. (In tab 3’s space–time picture, a collision would be two curves crossing at a point — exactly what never happens.)

Note — no collisions, in equations (three steps) 
   step 1 — reverse time; the field flips sign:
     Y_s := X_{t′−s}   ⇒   dY_s/ds = −Ẋ_{t′−s} = −u_{t′−s}(Y_s) ,   Y₀ = X_{t′}
     again a first-order IVP, field just as Lipschitz ⇒ uniqueness applies backwards:
     a point at time t′ has exactly ONE past — two particles sharing it would share
     that past, forcing x₀ = x₁.

   step 2 — the gap  Δ_t := ψ_t(x₀) − ψ_t(x₁)  obeys a two-sided bound:
     d/dt ‖Δ_t‖ ≤ ‖u_t(ψ_t(x₀)) − u_t(ψ_t(x₁))‖ ≤ L·‖Δ_t‖      (Lipschitz, tab 0)
     Grönwall, forward and (via step 1) backward:
          e^{−Lt}‖Δ₀‖  ≤  ‖Δ_t‖  ≤  e^{+Lt}‖Δ₀‖
     lower bound ⇒ ‖Δ_{t′}‖ ≥ e^{−Lt′}·‖x₀−x₁‖ > 0 :   gaps shrink at most
     exponentially and NEVER reach zero in finite time — ψ_{t′} is injective.
     (exact on the example: u = −x has L = 1 and Δ_t = e⁻ᵗΔ₀ — the contraction
      approaches 0 forever without arriving.)

   step 3 — the inverse is the backward flow:
     ψ_{t′}⁻¹(y) = solve dY/ds = −u_{t′−s}(Y) from Y₀ = y, for time t′
     example u(x) = −x:  backward field = +Y  ⇒  ψ₀.₇⁻¹(0.993) = 0.993·e^{0.7} = 2 ✓
     — bib 2 recovered, uniquely. This backward solve is exactly what a CNF runs
     to find a data point’s noise preimage (tab 2).

The linking equation — read the field at the destination

d/dt ψ_t(x_0) = u_t( ψ_t(x_0) )     equivalently   d/dt ψ_t = u_t ∘ ψ_t

Read: d/dt ψ_t(x_0) is the velocity of the particle when it is at its destination ψ_t(x_0) — so the field is read there. The composition symbol says only that: first find where the particle is, then read the arrow posted there.

Why not u_t(x_0)? Not a type error — x_0 is also a position. It is the wrong-time position: the particle’s spot at t = 0, stale by time t, surviving only as the particle’s permanent name (a bib number, assigned by the starting line; by uniqueness one start = one trajectory = one particle, forever). Arrows are posted at places, and at time t the arrow at location x_0 is obeyed by whoever stands there now — a different particle:

   ψ_t(x₀) = e⁻ᵗx₀ ,  u(x) = −x ,  t = 0.7 :
   ours (bib 2):   now at ψ₀.₇(2) = 0.993;   d/dt ψ_t(2) = −2e⁻⁰·⁷ = −0.993 = u(0.993) ✓
   u(x₀) = u(2) = −2   ✗ — the arrow at the OLD spot, now obeyed by bib 4.03 (= 2e⁺⁰·⁷):
   the correct velocity of the WRONG particle.      (only exception: t = 0, ψ₀ = id)
Note — places vs particles (Eulerian vs Lagrangian), in four lines

v_t(x_0) := d/dt ψ_t(x_0) — velocity by bib number — is a legitimate object (the Lagrangian description); the place-indexed u_t is the Eulerian one; the dictionary between them is exactly v_t = u_t ∘ ψ_t. The theory builds on u because: (1) dX/dt = u_t(X_t) can be written before any trajectory exists — per-particle velocities presuppose the flow, which is circular; (2) by uniqueness, two particles at the same place and time move identically, so given the current position the bib adds nothing; (3) u^θ(x,t) is evaluable anywhere in space — a per-particle velocity lives on one curve. Inverting instead of relabeling: x_0 = ψ_t⁻¹(x) ⇒ u_t(x) = ∂_t ψ_t(ψ_t⁻¹(x)) — and tab 3’s CondOT derivation is exactly this substitution.

A worked example you can differentiate live

Take the simplest interesting field: a linear contraction, u_t(x) = −θx ("velocity always points at the origin, proportional to distance"). Claim: the flow is exponential shrinkage, ψ_t(x_0) = e^{−θt} x_0. Verify by plugging into the ODE — this is the check an interviewer can watch you do:

   claim:    ψ_t(x₀) = e^{−θt} · x₀
   check 1:  ψ₀(x₀) = x₀                                   ✓ initial condition
   check 2:  d/dt ψ_t(x₀) = −θ·e^{−θt}·x₀ = −θ·ψ_t(x₀)
                          = u( ψ_t(x₀) )                    ✓ solves the ODE

   read it:  every point slides toward 0, covering a fixed FRACTION of the
   remaining distance per unit time — so a whole cloud of points shrinks
   toward the origin without ever crossing itself.

Why this tiny example earns its place: flow matching's own conditional field is exactly this field, re-centred and re-clocked. The CondOT target can be rewritten as u_t(x|z) = (z − x)/(1−t) (derived in tab 3) — a contraction toward the data point z whose rate 1/(1−t) speeds up as time runs out. Linear-in-x velocity is also why everything in the Gaussian-path story stays closed-form: linear fields have linear flows, and linear maps of Gaussians are Gaussians.

Flows move points — therefore they move distributions

Feed the ODE a random start X_0 ~ p_0 and the output X_t = ψ_t(X_0) is random too; its law is the pushforward p_t = [ψ_t]_♯ p_0. Two equivalent camera angles, worth naming:

  • Lagrangian (ride a particle): follow one trajectory X_t. This is what the ODE and the sampler talk about.
  • Eulerian (stand at a point): watch the density value p_t(x) at a fixed x rise and fall as mass flows past. This is what "the model transports N(0,I) to p_data" talks about.
   the running example, now with a RANDOM start:
   X₀ ~ N(0, 1),   ψ_t(x₀) = e^{−t}·x₀   ⇒   X_t = e^{−t}·X₀  ~  N(0, e^{−2t})

   Lagrangian view: every particle slides toward 0.
   Eulerian view:   the Gaussian narrows,  p_t = N(0, e^{−2t}).
   (a LINEAR flow maps Gaussians to Gaussians — the same fact the FM path lives on)

The bookkeeping that ties the two views together is conservation of probability mass — none is created or destroyed, only carried by the field:

∂_t p_t(x) = −div( p_t u_t )(x)     (continuity equation)

Read it as a fluid statement: the density at x changes exactly by the net inflow of mass that the velocity field carries through x (div = net outflow rate, hence the minus sign). A field u generates a density path p_t if and only if the pair satisfies this PDE — it is the certificate we will cash in twice: tab 2's likelihood formula is it evaluated along a trajectory, and tab 3's marginalization trick is a three-line computation with it.

A flow model

Make the vector field a neural network u_t^θ(x) that takes (x, t) and returns a velocity. Then:

   sample   X_0 ~ N(0, I)                 ← the only randomness

2. Continuous normalizing flows — the right model, trained the wrong way

One paragraph of (discrete) normalizing flows

A normalizing flow generates by pushing noise through an invertible map f and trains by maximum likelihood via the change-of-variables formula: log p(x) = log p_0(f⁻¹(x)) + log|det J_{f⁻¹}(x)|. The whole engineering game is designing layers whose inverse and Jacobian determinant are cheap (coupling layers etc.), which caps expressivity.

The CNF idea (Neural ODE paper, Chen et al. 2018): det becomes trace

Replace the stack of discrete invertible layers with an ODE — a "continuous-depth" flow. The flow map ψ_t is automatically invertible (run the ODE backwards). And the change-of-variables formula turns from a determinant into a trace:

d/dt  log p_t(X_t)  =  −tr( ∂u_t/∂x (X_t) )      (instantaneous change of variables)
   log p_1(x_1)  =  log p_0(x_0)  −  ∫₀¹ tr( ∂u_t/∂x (X_t) ) dt

   read it as:  final log-density
              = easy Gaussian log-density at the trajectory's start
              − how much the field expanded volume along the way
   (divergence tr(∂u/∂x) > 0 = field spreads points out = density drops)
Note — where the trace comes from (30-second derivation) 
   one Euler step of the flow:    x  ↦  x + h·u_t(x)
   its Jacobian:                  J_step = I + h·(∂u/∂x)
   det(I + h·A) = 1 + h·tr(A) + O(h²)     (off-diagonal products enter at h²)
   ⇒  log|det J_step| = h·tr(∂u/∂x) + O(h²)

   the full flow = composition of 1/h such steps; log-dets ADD:
   log|det J_ψ|  =  Σ h·tr(∂u/∂x)  ──h→0──▶  ∫₀¹ tr(∂u/∂x) dt

So the determinant of a product of nearly-identity Jacobians melts into an integral of traces. And tr(∂u/∂x) = div(u) is the field's local volume-growth rate (tab 1's continuity equation, evaluated along a trajectory): positive divergence spreads nearby particles apart, volume grows, density drops — that's the minus sign in the formula above.

   sanity-check on the running example u_t(x) = −x  (1-D, so tr = the derivative):
   tr(∂u/∂x) = −1     ⇒  formula says: log-density along a trajectory GROWS at rate 1
   directly:  p_t = N(0, e^{−2t})   ⇒   p_t(0) = e^{t}/√(2π)   ⇒   d/dt log p_t(0) = 1  ✓
   (the field compresses everything toward 0, so density there rises — signs agree)

Interview line: "in continuous time the log-det of a composition of infinitely many infinitesimal steps collapses to an integral of the trace — and a trace is much cheaper than a determinant, and puts no architectural constraint on u." That was the promise: free-form architectures with exact likelihoods.

The CNF training loop — where it goes wrong

Train by maximum likelihood: for every training example, on every step you must know where its trajectory came from and how much volume changed. That means solving the ODE inside the loss:

   CNF training step (2018)                FM training step (2023, tab 3)
   ─────────────────────────────           ─────────────────────────────
   x  = data point                         z = data,  ε ~ N(0,I),  t ~ U[0,1]
   solve ODE x → x_0        (50–100        x_t    = t·z + (1−t)·ε     (1 line)
     network evals, sequential)            target = z − ε             (1 line)
   accumulate ∫ tr(∂u/∂x) dt along         loss = ‖u_θ(x_t, t) − target‖²
     the way (trace per eval)                                     (1 eval)
   loss = −log p₀(x₀) + ∫ tr(…) dt
   backprop through the whole solve        backprop through 1 eval

The three costs (say all three)

  • Simulation in the loop. Each training step is a full sequential ODE solve — 50–100+ network evals that cannot be parallelized over time. And as training progresses the learned field tends to get stiffer, so the adaptive solver takes more steps.
  • The trace. Exact tr(∂u/∂x) costs d backprops (one per dimension). FFJORD replaces it with Hutchinson's stochastic estimator tr(A) = E[εᵗAε] — one backprop, but now the likelihood itself is noisy.
  • Backprop through the solver. Either store every intermediate state (memory ~ NFE) or use the adjoint method (solve a second ODE backwards for gradients — constant memory, but yet more sequential solves and its own numerical error).
The verdict, one sentence

CNFs picked the right model (a learned ODE from noise to data) and the wrong training signal (likelihood, which is only computable by simulating). Flow matching keeps the model and changes the signal to something available in closed form before any simulation happens.

The flip

The CNF asks: given my current network, what path does each data point take, and how likely is it? — answerable only by simulation. Flow matching flips it: I will decide the path myself, per data point, in advance. Then the velocity along that path is known in closed form, and training is supervised regression onto it. Four steps.

Step 1 — pick a conditional probability path

Condition on one data point z. Choose any interpolation p_t(·|z) with p_0(·|z) = N(0,I) and p_1(·|z) = δ_z (all mass on z). The standard choice is the Gaussian path, and its simplest instance is CondOT — literally the straight line:

p_t(x|z) = N(α_t z, β_t² I)    sample:  x_t = α_t z + β_t ε    CondOT:  α_t = t, β_t = 1−t  ⇒  x_t = t·z + (1−t)·ε

Averaging over the dataset gives the marginal path p_t(x) = ∫ p_t(x|z) p_data(z) dz, which interpolates the full distributions: p_0 = N(0,I), p_1 = p_data. You can sample from it trivially (draw z, draw ε, interpolate); you can never evaluate its density — and you won't need to.

Step 2 — the conditional velocity is known in closed form

What velocity field carries a particle along that path toward z? For the Gaussian path (derivation in the note below — a classic whiteboard ask):

u_t(x|z) = ( α̇_t − (β̇_t/β_t)α_t ) z + (β̇_t/β_t) x     CondOT:  u_t(x_t|z) = z − ε
Note — the two-line derivation (whiteboard-ready) 
   the path, written as a flow:   ψ_t(ε|z) = α_t·z + β_t·ε      (carries ε → z)
   velocity = its time derivative:  ψ̇_t    = α̇_t·z + β̇_t·ε

   express in terms of the CURRENT point x = ψ_t(ε|z), i.e. ε = (x − α_t z)/β_t :

     u_t(x|z) = α̇_t·z + (β̇_t/β_t)·(x − α_t z)
              = ( α̇_t − (β̇_t/β_t)·α_t )·z + (β̇_t/β_t)·x           ← general Gaussian path

   CondOT (α=t, β=1−t, α̇=1, β̇=−1):
     u_t(x|z) = (z − x)/(1 − t)      and on the path this equals   z − ε

Read the CondOT form: at time t you sit at x; the leftover distance is z−x and the leftover time is 1−t — velocity is distance over time. It is tab 1's linear contraction aimed at z, accelerating as the deadline t=1 approaches. Both forms matter: z−ε is what you type in the training loop (endpoints are in hand); (z−x)/(1−t) defines the field everywhere, which the demo below and the marginal-field formula need.

For CondOT the answer is the obvious one: a particle traversing a straight line from ε to z in unit time moves with constant velocity z − ε. That is the regression target. Memorize the pair:

The two lines the whole method compresses to

x_t = t·z + (1−t)·ε      target = z − ε

   plug in numbers:   z = 2,   ε = −1,   t = 0.25
   x_t    = 0.25·2 + 0.75·(−1) = −0.25
   target = z − ε              =  3
   cross-check the other form:  (z − x_t)/(1−t) = (2 − (−0.25))/0.75 = 3  ✓
   one pair, one point, one arrow — that is a single training example, in full.

Step 3 — marginalize: why regressing on per-point targets works at all

Different (z, ε) pairs put mass on the same point x_t with different targets. The network gets x_t and t only — it cannot know which z it came from. So what does MSE regression converge to? Its minimizer: the conditional expectation of the target given the input. That is exactly the marginal vector field:

u_t(x) = E[ u_t(x_t|z) | x_t = x ] = ∫ u_t(x|z) · p_t(x|z)p_data(z)/p_t(x) dz
        ε₁ ●──────╲   target z₂−ε₁                two paths cross at x_t
                   ╲ ╱                            with different targets;
                    ✕  ← x_t : net sees only this  the net can't output both.
                   ╱ ╲                            MSE optimum at x_t
        ε₂ ●──────╱   target z₁−ε₂                = posterior-weighted AVERAGE
       (z₁, z₂ on the other side)                 of the arrows = marginal u_t(x)

And the marginalization trick (proved via the continuity equation) says this averaged field is precisely the one whose ODE transports N(0,I) to p_data. Intuition: at x, average over "which z might I be flowing toward, given I'm at x now" — a posterior-weighted mean of arrows — and following that mean field moves the whole density correctly even though no individual particle targets a specific z anymore.

Smallest numeric case of the average: two data points z = ±1, equally likely, and you sit exactly at x = 0. By symmetry the posterior weights are ½ each, so the marginal field averages the two arrows (+1 − 0)/(1−t) and (−1 − 0)/(1−t) to exactly 0: at the ambiguous midpoint the field stalls, and it only commits once x drifts off-centre and one weight wins. The demo's magenta curves do precisely this near t = 0 — flat first, then a decisive bend.

Note — the continuity equation is the proof tool

Tab 1's mass-conservation PDE, ∂_t p_t = −div(p_t u_t), is what makes the marginalization trick a theorem rather than a hope. The check is three lines: differentiate p_t(x) = ∫ p_t(x|z) p_data(z) dz under the integral; each conditional pair (p_t(·|z), u_t(·|z)) satisfies continuity by construction, so substitute; pull the divergence out by linearity — and the posterior-averaged field pops out as the thing paired with the marginal density. Averaged field + marginal path satisfy continuity ⇒ the averaged field transports N(0,I) to p_data. No simulation appears anywhere in the argument, which is the entire point.

Step 4 — the CFM theorem (the one theorem)

The loss you want (regress on the intractable marginal field) and the loss you can compute (regress on the closed-form conditional field) differ by a constant that doesn't depend on θ:

   L_FM(θ)  = E_{t, x~p_t}        ‖u_θ(x,t) − u_t(x)‖²        ← intractable target
   L_CFM(θ) = E_{t, z, x~p_t(·|z)} ‖u_θ(x,t) − u_t(x|z)‖²      ← closed-form target

   Theorem:   L_FM(θ) = L_CFM(θ) + C     ⇒     ∇_θ L_FM = ∇_θ L_CFM

Proof shape (enough for an interview): expand both squares; the ‖u_θ‖² terms are identical; the cross terms ⟨u_θ, u_t(x)⟩ and ⟨u_θ, u_t(x|z)⟩ coincide after writing u_t(x) as its posterior average over z and swapping the order of integration; everything left over is θ-free. Same structure as the "denoising" trick in score matching.

The algorithm (this is all of it)

   train, per minibatch:                     sample, once trained:
     z  ~ dataset                              x ~ N(0, I)
     t  ~ Uniform[0,1]   (per example!)        for n Euler steps:
     ε  ~ N(0, I)                                  x ← x + h · u_θ(x, t)
     x_t = t·z + (1−t)·ε                       return x
     loss = ‖u_θ(x_t, t) − (z − ε)‖²
     backprop

Three properties to name: simulation-free (no ODE during training — the entire CNF pathology is gone), a plain regression (stable MSE, no adversarial game, no ELBO), and path-agnostic (swap α_t, β_t and the same code trains any Gaussian path — including diffusion's, tab 5). This recipe trains Stable Diffusion 3, Movie Gen, and Voicebox.

The convention Voicebox / Lipman et al. use

The original FM paper (and Voicebox's background section) writes the OT path with a floor σ_min so the endpoint is a sharp Gaussian around z rather than exactly δ_z:

x_t = (1 − (1−σ_min)t)·x_0 + t·x_1    u_t = x_1 − (1−σ_min)·x_0    (x_0 = noise, x_1 = data, σ_min ≈ 10⁻⁵)

Set σ_min = 0 and you recover x_t = t·x_1 + (1−t)·x_0, target x_1 − x_0. Same method, cosmetic difference — but check which convention a paper uses before quoting its equations.

Trap — the loss does not go to zero

At a crossing point x_t the best possible output is the average of several different targets, so residual variance stays in the loss forever. A flow-matching loss that plateaus well above zero is converged, not broken. (Corollary: the loss value is a poor model-quality metric; monitor samples.)

def fm_loss(model, z):                       # z: (B, d) minibatch of data
    B   = z.shape[0]
    t   = torch.rand(B, device=z.device)     # (B,)  one t PER example
    eps = torch.randn_like(z)                # noise endpoint
    xt  = t[:, None] * z + (1 - t[:, None]) * eps   # point on the line
    target = z - eps                         # constant velocity of that line

5. Diffusion ↔ flow matching — one family, two parameterizations

The score function

Diffusion models organize everything around the score ∇_x log p_t(x) — the direction of steepest ascent in log-density ("which way is more probable"). For the Gaussian conditional path it is closed-form: ∇ log p_t(x|z) = −(x − α_t z)/β_t², i.e. essentially "−ε, rescaled" — which is why DDPM's noise-prediction loss ‖ε_θ(x_t) − ε‖² is denoising score matching with the constants dropped, via ε_θ = −β_t s_θ.

The conversion formula — train one, get the other free

On a Gaussian path, velocity and score are both linear in (x, z), so each is an affine function of the other:

u_t(x) = a_t ∇log p_t(x) + b_t x    a_t = β_t²(α̇_t/α_t) − β̇_tβ_t    b_t = α̇_t/α_t

Deeper way to say it: velocity, score, noise-prediction ε̂, and the denoiser E[z|x_t] are all linear reparameterizations of the same learned object — the posterior mean of clean data given the noisy point. "Flow matching model" vs "diffusion model" names the output head, not a different animal.

Same marginals, deterministic or stochastic sampler

The trained ODE can be turned into an SDE with identical marginals p_t by adding Langevin churn built from the score (proved with the Fokker–Planck equation, which is the continuity equation plus a diffusion term):

dX_t = [ u_t(X_t) + (σ_t²/2) ∇log p_t(X_t) ] dt + σ_t dW_t    for ANY σ_t ≥ 0
   σ_t = 0 :  flow / probability-flow ODE   smooth deterministic paths
   σ_t > 0 :  diffusion-style sampling      zig-zag paths, same p_t at every t

   one trained network  ⇒  a whole dial of samplers, chosen AFTER training
   (in practice a mildly stochastic sampler can correct accumulated error)

Classifier-free guidance — the 4-line derivation

Guidance is a score-space argument, which is why it lives in this tab. Samples don't adhere to the prompt y strongly enough → sharpen the part of the score that "listens" to y:

   Bayes on the score:  ∇log p_t(x|y) = ∇log p_t(x) + ∇log p_t(y|x)
                                          ↑ unguided     ↑ "classifier" term

   scale the classifier by w > 1:   ∇log p_t(x) + w·∇log p_t(y|x)     (classifier guidance —
                                                          needs a separate noisy classifier)
   eliminate it by running Bayes backwards, ∇log p_t(y|x) = ∇log p_t(x|y) − ∇log p_t(x):

        = ∇log p_t(x) + w·( ∇log p_t(x|y) − ∇log p_t(x) )
        = (1−w)·∇log p_t(x)  +  w·∇log p_t(x|y)

   score ↔ velocity is affine (formula above)  ⇒  identical combination for velocities:

        ũ_t(x|y) = (1−w)·u_t(x|∅) + w·u_t(x|y)

Both fields come from one network trained with label dropout (tab 4's p_drop): the ∅ token plays ∇log p_t(x). Three things to say: w=1 is the honest conditional model; w>1 no longer samples exactly p(x|y) — it's a heuristic that over-sharpens toward the prompt, kept because it works; and it costs two network evals per step.

Note — tab 4's trainer becomes a diffusion-path trainer with a 3-line edit
swap the schedule inside fm_loss
a,  b  = torch.sin(0.5 * math.pi * t), torch.cos(0.5 * math.pi * t)  # VP cosine path
da, db = 0.5 * math.pi * b,           -0.5 * math.pi * a             # their d/dt
xt     = a[:, None] * z + b[:, None] * eps
target = da[:, None] * z + db[:, None] * eps          # general rule: ẋ_t = α̇z + β̇ε

Same trainer, same sampler, same network — you have merely chosen diffusion's curved variance-preserving path (α²+β²=1) instead of the straight one. This is the concrete sense in which "FM vs diffusion" is a choice of (α_t, β_t) and output head, not of algorithm.

The one-sentence answer to "is flow matching just diffusion?"

Same family, different coordinates. Gaussian-path flow matching and diffusion are inter-convertible — velocity ↔ score by an affine formula, ODE ↔ SDE with identical marginals. What FM changes in practice is the default path (straight, so fewer sampling steps) and the training frame (velocity regression), which is why SD3, Movie Gen and Voicebox train with FM.

What it is, in one line

A non-autoregressive speech generator trained with flow matching on text-guided speech infilling: given surrounding audio and a transcript, fill in the missing (masked) speech. Framed this way, one model does zero-shot TTS, style transfer, noise removal, and content editing — the same "train on masked infilling, prompt at inference" move that made GPT-style in-context learning work, ported to speech.

The training task

   inputs :  y      phoneme sequence (frame-aligned transcript)
             x_ctx  mel spectrogram with a random span MASKED
             x_t    noisy mel  = (1−(1−σ_min)t)·x_0 + t·x_1,   x_0 ~ N(0,I)
   model  :  Transformer  u_θ(x_t, x_ctx, y, t)  → velocity, mel-shaped
   loss   :  ‖u_θ(...) − (x_1 − (1−σ_min)·x_0)‖²   only on MASKED frames

   zero-shot TTS at inference = infilling with the 3-second enrollment
   clip as unmasked context: the model continues in that voice.

Exactly tab 3's recipe — the OT path with σ_min, conditioned on (x_ctx, y). Nothing about FM changes; conditioning is just extra network inputs.

The stack (two FM/regression models + a vocoder)

  • Audio model: Transformer over 80-dim log-mel frames; x_t, x_ctx are concatenated frame-wise, phoneme embeddings are added per frame. Trained with the CFM loss above on masked spans.
  • Duration model: same infilling idea on per-phoneme durations (predict masked durations from context durations + phonemes) — needed to stretch the transcript to frame alignment before the audio model runs. A simple regression version works about as well as the FM version.
  • Vocoder: HiFi-GAN turns generated mel back into a waveform.
  • Data: tens of thousands of hours of audiobook speech (≈60K h English; ≈50K h across six languages for the multilingual model) — scale that in-the-loop-simulation training could not have touched.

Classifier-free guidance + inference

Drop the conditioning (x_ctx, y) with some probability during training; at inference push toward the conditional field:

ũ = u_θ(x_t, t) + w · ( u_θ(x_t, x_ctx, y, t) − u_θ(x_t, t) )

Sampling: draw mel-shaped Gaussian noise, solve the ODE (midpoint solver) to t=1, vocode. Two dials to quote: NFE trades quality against speed (usable speech at very low NFE, saturating by a few dozen), and CFG doubles the cost per step (two network evals). Because generation is one parallel ODE solve over all frames rather than a token-by-token loop, Voicebox beat the autoregressive VALL-E on intelligibility and similarity while being up to ~20× faster.

Why flow matching fits speech (the "why this design" question)

  • Non-autoregressive: mel frames come out in parallel; latency scales with NFE, not utterance length — and infilling needs bidirectional context anyway, which AR models can't see.
  • Continuous data: spectrogram frames are naturally continuous vectors — the R^d setting FM is built for, no tokenizer/quantizer in the generator's way.
  • Stable, scalable training: one MSE, no adversarial game, no simulation — safe to scale to 60K hours.
  • Controllable inference: quality↔speed via NFE and adherence via CFG are post-training knobs, per tab 5.

Why doesn't the FM loss reach zero?At a crossing point the targets from different (z,ε) pairs differ; MSE's optimum is their mean, leaving irreducible variance in the loss.
Can an FM model give likelihoods?Yes, post-hoc: integrate −tr(∂u/∂x) along the sampling ODE (the CNF formula). You just never pay that cost during training.
Why do straight conditional paths help?They make the marginal field less curved, so coarse Euler/midpoint steps stay accurate → low NFE at a given quality.
Is sampling deterministic?Given X_0, yes — it's an ODE; all randomness lives in X_0. Want stochasticity? Add score-scaled Langevin noise: same marginals (tab 5).
What does the net equal at convergence?E[z − ε | x_t, t] — the posterior-mean velocity; equivalently an affine function of the denoiser E[z|x_t] and of the score.
z−ε vs (z−x_t)/(1−t)?Identical on the path; the second form defines the field off-path and reveals it as a contraction toward z (remaining distance ÷ remaining time).
Why Gaussian p_init?Anything you can sample works — FM never needs p_init's density during training; Gaussian just keeps the path/score algebra closed-form.
Single t per batch — what breaks?Each gradient step sees one time slice: high inter-step variance, poor coverage of t, visibly worse convergence. Sample t per example.

The 60-second drill

Say this out loud before the interview
  1. A flow model is an ODE: sample Gaussian noise, integrate a learned velocity field u_θ(x,t) from t=0 to 1, land on data. The network is the velocity; sampling is the integration.
  2. CNFs trained this by maximum likelihood via d log p/dt = −tr(∂u/∂x) — which forces an ODE solve plus a trace integral inside every training step. Right model, unscalable training signal.
  3. Flow matching chooses the path per data point instead: x_t = tz + (1−t)ε, whose velocity is the constant z − ε. Train by MSE regression on it — one network eval, no simulation.
  4. Why it's the right target: MSE's minimizer is the conditional expectation, so the net learns the posterior-average velocity — exactly the marginal field that transports noise-density to data-density (CFM theorem: same gradients as the intractable loss).
  5. Diffusion is the same family in score coordinates: on Gaussian paths velocity ↔ score by an affine map, and adding (σ²/2)∇log p Langevin churn gives an SDE sampler with identical marginals. FM's practical edge: straight paths, fewer steps.
  6. Voicebox = this recipe on speech: FM on masked mel infilling conditioned on phonemes + context, a duration model, CFG, midpoint ODE solve, HiFi-GAN — non-autoregressive, so ~20× faster than VALL-E at better quality.
